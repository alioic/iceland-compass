Whale Watching in Iceland: The Complete Guide to the Best Spots

Everything about whale watching in Iceland: which whales you can see, the best locations, timing, tours, seasickness tips and responsible operators.

Why Iceland is a world-class whale-watching destination

Few places on Earth offer whale watching as reliable as Iceland, and that is no accident. The cold, nutrient-rich seas around the island are among the most productive anywhere. Where warm and cold ocean currents meet, they trigger a huge bloom of plankton, which in turn draws in the small fish, sand eels, capelin, herring and mackerel that whales feed on. It is an enormous buffet, and it is why whales gather in the bays and fjords all around the country in summer.

On top of that come the bright summer nights. When the sun barely sets at the height of summer, tours can run late into the evening on calm seas with excellent visibility. Many boats sail from morning until night during peak season, and the conditions for spotting animals are often as good as they get anywhere in the world.

Success rates here are among the highest you will find. Over the summer months, many operators report seeing whales on roughly nine out of ten trips, and it is common to be offered a second trip at no charge if nothing shows up. This combination of nutrient-rich water, long bright nights and consistently high success makes Iceland one of the most sought-after whale-watching destinations on the planet.

Which whales and marine animals you can see

The star of Icelandic whale watching is the humpback whale (hnúfubakur). Curious and often close to boats, it truly puts on a show: breaching clear of the water, slapping its tail and its long pectoral fins in displays no one forgets. Humpbacks are common in the north and are seen regularly from Reykjavík in summer too, and for many visitors they are the highlight of the trip.

The minke whale (hrefna) is also very common. Smaller and more understated than the humpback, it tends to surface briefly and dive again, and it is the whale most people see on shorter trips, especially out of Faxaflói bay. The harbour porpoise (hnísa), the smallest cetacean around Iceland, is often seen in small groups but is shyer and harder to keep in view.

White-beaked dolphins (hnýðingur, also just called höfrungar, dolphins) are a crowd favourite. They travel in lively pods, leaping and playing in the wake of the boats, and can number in the dozens. Rarer but still possible are the sperm whale (búrhvalur) and, most thrilling of all, the blue whale (steypireyður), the largest animal ever to have lived. Blue whales are sometimes seen in summer, especially off Húsavík, but that is luck rather than a given.

The orca or killer whale (háhyrningur) holds a special place. These powerful predators are best seen around Snæfellsnes in late winter and spring, when they follow the herring into the fjords. In winter this is some of the finest orca watching available anywhere. Be realistic: humpback, minke, porpoise and white-beaked dolphins are what you will most often see, while blue whale, sperm whale and orca are rarer, seasonal visitors.

Húsavík – the whale-watching capital

Húsavík, on Skjálfandi bay in the north, is often called the whale-watching capital of Iceland, and frequently of Europe as well. Skjálfandi (a broad northern bay) is wide and nutrient-rich, and success rates here are among the very highest known. Through the summer, seeing a whale is close to guaranteed, and the variety is impressive: humpbacks are frequent, minke whales common, and now and then the blue whale itself appears.

The atmosphere in Húsavík is special. Many tours are run on beautifully restored oak boats, old shark- and herring-fishing vessels that lend the experience a warm, traditional feel. The town is also home to the Húsavík Whale Museum (Hvalasafnið), where you can learn about whale biology and the history of whaling before heading out, which many find the perfect way to prepare for the trip.

Húsavík is farther from Reykjavík, roughly a five- to six-hour drive, or a short flight to Akureyri and onward from there. But for anyone who puts success rate and quality of experience first, Húsavík is hard to beat.

Akureyri and Eyjafjörður

Akureyri, the largest town in the north, sits at the head of Eyjafjörður (the country's longest fjord). Being long and sheltered, the fjord often stays calm even when it blows hard out at sea, which makes the trips comfortable. Humpback and minke whales are seen here regularly through the summer, and white-beaked dolphins often come to play.

One of Eyjafjörður's advantages is that whale watching is possible here in winter too, which is rarer outside Reykjavík. The shelter of the fjord makes winter trips feasible when the weather allows, and seeing whales framed by snow-covered mountains is unforgettable. Akureyri is also well connected, with flights from Reykjavík and good services, which makes it a convenient base for whale watching in the north.

Reykjavík – the most convenient option

From the Old Harbour in Reykjavík, boats head out onto Faxaflói bay, and for many visitors this is the simplest and most accessible option. There is no long journey to make; you can add whale watching directly onto a city stay, even on the same day as other plans.

On Faxaflói you will most often see minke and humpback whales, along with harbour porpoises and white-beaked dolphins. Success is good over the summer, though typically a touch lower than in the north. Reykjavík's biggest advantage is that it sails year-round, which few other places offer. Winter trips are quieter, colder and more weather-dependent, but they can be magical.

If time is short, or you would rather not build your trip around the north, Reykjavík is an excellent choice. You give up a little in success rate and variety in exchange for real convenience.

Snæfellsnes – orcas in winter

In the west of the country, out of Ólafsvík and Grundarfjörður on the Snæfellsnes peninsula, something special happens in the coldest months. When the herring gather in the fjords the orcas follow, and from late winter into spring, roughly February to April, this is one of the best places in the country to see these magnificent animals.

The spectacle can be extraordinary: whole pods of orcas hunting, sometimes alongside humpbacks and clouds of seabirds chasing the same herring. Grundarfjörður, with its famous Kirkjufell mountain in the background, is a particularly photogenic place for such a trip.

Bear in mind that this is a winter experience. The weather is cold, the days are short, and conditions can change plans, so flexibility pays off. But for anyone who especially wants to see orcas, Snæfellsnes is the right place at the right time.

Dalvík and smaller spots

Dalvík, on Eyjafjörður, offers a more personal, homely style of whale watching. The boats are smaller, the groups more intimate and the mood relaxed, which some find gives a closer, more genuine experience than the large vessels.

Many trips from Dalvík combine whale watching with sea angling, where you can drop a line for fish and even grill your catch on board afterwards. It is a fun way to take in the maritime culture of the north in a single outing. Other smaller spots around the country likewise run trips by season, and they often mean fewer crowds and greater closeness to both the crew and the nature around you.

The best time of year for whale watching

The peak season is June to August, when the sea is calmest, the days are longest and whales are most numerous in the bays and fjords feeding up for winter. This is when success rates are highest, and often puffins join the picture too, because from May to mid-August you can combine whale watching with puffin watching.

The broader season runs roughly from April into October. In the shoulder months there are fewer departures and success is a little lower, but it can be quieter and prices sometimes more favourable. Reykjavík stands apart with year-round sailings, though winter trips are more weather-dependent.

Orca watching around Snæfellsnes, on the other hand, is a winter and spring event, roughly February to April. So you could say that some form of whale watching is possible in Iceland all year round, but if you want to maximise both success and variety, aim for high summer.

Húsavík or Reykjavík – an honest comparison

This is the most common question, and the answer depends on what matters most to you. Húsavík wins on success rate and experience. Skjálfandi is one of the most productive bays in the country, the variety is greater, and the traditional oak boats together with the Whale Museum make for a more complete day. If whale watching is one of the main goals of your trip, the journey north is worth it.

Reykjavík wins on convenience. You sail straight from the city centre with no long trip, you can go year-round, and you can slot it easily into a city break. Success is good over the summer, though typically a little lower, and the variety is somewhat narrower than in the north.

A simple rule of thumb: if you are in the north anyway, or you want the best possible experience, choose Húsavík. If your time is limited or you want simplicity, Reykjavík is an excellent choice that rarely disappoints.

Combining whales with puffins

From early May to mid-August, the puffin (lundi) nests on islands and cliffs around the country, and many whale-watching trips combine both in a single sailing. From Reykjavík, for instance, boats often pass puffin colonies on islands such as Akurey and Lundey on the way out to the bay.

The puffin is one of Iceland's best-loved birds, colourful and endlessly entertaining to watch, and seeing thousands of them in flight and bobbing on the water around the boat is an experience in itself. Some trips are designed specifically as combined whale-and-puffin tours on smaller, faster boats that can get closer to the islands. If you are travelling in spring or early summer, it is well worth asking about these tours and getting two of nature's highlights in one sailing.

What a tour is like

A typical whale-watching tour lasts around two to three and a half hours, depending on the location and type of boat. Once you are on board, the crew heads for the areas where animals have recently been seen, and a guide talks about the marine life, whale behaviour and whatever comes into view along the way.

There are two main types of boat. The larger boats are stable, with indoor space, toilets and often refreshments, and suit families and anyone who wants comfort. The small, fast inflatable speedboats, often called RIBs, are more of an adventure: they go faster and get closer, but they are more exposed to wind and spray and are less suited to very young or unsteady passengers.

Most operators lend out warm overalls on board, which make a real difference because it is always colder at sea than on land. Still, bring an extra layer, a hat and gloves, a camera and ideally binoculars. Remember that even on a sunny day it can be bitterly cold out on the open water.

Seasickness and how to avoid it

Seasickness can spoil a trip, but with a few simple steps it can usually be kept under control. The single most effective measure is to take motion-sickness medication in good time, ideally about an hour before departure, since these medicines work best as prevention and poorly once the nausea has started. Natural remedies such as ginger and pressure wristbands work well for some people.

On board, look out toward the horizon rather than down at the boat or your phone, as this helps your brain reconcile motion and vision. Get fresh air out on the open deck and stay toward the middle of the boat, where the movement is least. Eat a light meal before the trip, avoid alcohol the night before, and drink water. Keeping warm matters too, because cold and wet conditions make queasiness worse. With these steps, most people have a fine trip even when there is a bit of a swell.

Responsible and ethical whale watching

Whale watching should happen on the animals' terms. Responsible operators follow a code of conduct, including the one championed by the IceWhale association, which emphasises keeping a reasonable distance, not chasing the whales, not driving at them and not disturbing their behaviour. When choosing a tour, it is worth asking whether the operator follows such a code, as it protects both the welfare of the animals and the quality of the experience over the long term.

It is right to address the whaling issue honestly. Iceland is one of very few nations that has continued some commercial whaling, mainly of fin whales, and it has long been controversial both at home and internationally. The scale of this hunting has been declining in recent years.

It is important to understand that whale watching is an entirely separate industry. It is built on protecting whales and enjoying them alive, and today it is a far larger and more valuable industry than whaling. By choosing a responsible whale-watching tour, you support the conservation-friendly use and protection of these animals.

Practical booking notes

During peak season, especially July and August, the most popular trips often fill up, so it pays to book ahead, particularly if you want a specific day, time or type of boat. Outside peak season there is more room, but the weather is then more likely to interfere.

Keep in mind that trips are sometimes cancelled due to weather, and most operators will then offer a rebooking or refund, so build a little flexibility into your itinerary. Check whether your chosen trip offers a free second sailing if no whales are seen, which many do over the summer.

And last but not least: dress warmer than you think you need to. It is a classic mistake to underestimate the cold out on the open water. Warm, windproof layers, a hat, gloves and sturdy shoes make the difference between fully enjoying the trip and counting the minutes until you are back on land.

Frequently asked questions

When is the best time to go whale watching in Iceland?

Peak season is June to August, when the sea is calmest, the days are longest and success rates are highest. The broader season runs roughly April to October. Reykjavík sails year-round. If you want to see orcas, aim for Snæfellsnes in late winter and spring, roughly February to April.

Which whales can I expect to see?

The most common are the humpback whale (hnúfubakur), minke whale (hrefna), harbour porpoise (hnísa) and white-beaked dolphins (hnýðingur). Rarer, seasonal sightings include the blue whale (steypireyður, sometimes off Húsavík), orca (háhyrningur, best around Snæfellsnes in winter) and sperm whale (búrhvalur). The humpback is the star, famous for breaching and tail-slapping.

Húsavík or Reykjavík – which is better?

Húsavík on Skjálfandi bay has the highest success rate, the most variety and traditional oak boats, but is farther from Reykjavík. Reykjavík is the most convenient, sails year-round and slots easily into a city stay, but success and variety are typically a little lower. For the best experience choose Húsavík; for convenience choose Reykjavík.

How likely am I to see a whale?

Success rates are very high over the summer. Many operators report whale sightings on roughly nine out of ten trips in peak season, and it is common to be offered a second trip at no charge if nothing shows up. Outside summer success is a little lower but often still good, especially in the north.

What should I wear and bring?

Dress warmer than you think you need to. Warm, windproof layers, a hat, gloves and sturdy shoes are essential, because it is always colder at sea than on land. Most boats lend out warm overalls. Also bring a camera with a zoom lens and ideally binoculars.

How long is a whale-watching tour?

A typical tour lasts around two to three and a half hours, depending on the location and type of boat. Larger boats are stable and comfortable with indoor space, while small fast inflatable speedboats (RIBs) get closer and faster but are more exposed to wind and spray.

What can I do about seasickness?

Take motion-sickness medication in good time, ideally about an hour before departure, as it works best as prevention. On board, look toward the horizon, get fresh air on deck and stay toward the middle of the boat. Eat lightly before the trip, avoid alcohol the night before, and keep warm.

Can I see puffins on the same trip?

Yes. From early May to mid-August many trips combine whale and puffin watching. From Reykjavík, for example, boats often pass puffin colonies on islands such as Akurey and Lundey. Ask about combined tours if you are travelling in spring or early summer.

Is whale watching ethical given that whaling still happens?

Yes. Whale watching is a separate industry built on enjoying whales alive and protecting them, and today it is far larger than whaling. Iceland is one of very few nations that has continued some whaling, mainly of fin whales, but it is controversial and has been declining. Choose an operator that follows a responsible whale-watching code of conduct.
