Bathing as the heart of Icelandic culture

Few things are as Icelandic as sitting in hot water. Long before tourists came to soak in a milky blue lagoon, the bath was part of everyday life: the place where people meet, debate the news, unwind after work, and let the tension melt away in a hot tub while the snow falls. Iceland is rich in geothermal energy, which means hot water is not a luxury but an everyday given – it flows from the tap and fills swimming pools in every single town.

For a visitor the vocabulary can be confusing, because Icelandic bathing culture comes in several forms. It pays to understand four distinct experiences before you set off, because price, atmosphere and access are completely different.

First, the public swimming pool (sundlaug) – the cheapest, most local and often most enjoyable experience, where locals show up daily. Second, the premium lagoons and geothermal spas – a designed experience with an entry fee, shower facilities, a bar and often a restaurant; the Blue Lagoon is the most famous. Third, nature baths – man-made pools that harness geothermal heat but are simpler and blend into the landscape. And fourth, the wild, free hot springs and natural pools – geothermal pools out in nature that no one charges for, but which demand caution and respect. This guide covers them all.

Pool, lagoon, nature bath or wild spring – what is the difference?

A sundlaug is a chlorinated public swimming pool with lanes, hot tubs (heitir pottar), often a steam room and sometimes slides. Almost every town in Iceland runs one, and the entry fee is low. This is where you meet the real Iceland, not the staged version.

Lagoons and geothermal spas are the premium experience. The water is geothermal water or geothermal seawater, often mineral-rich, and the setting is designed: infinity edges, in-water bars, steam rooms and views. The entry fee is many times higher than a pool, and the most popular ones must be booked in advance.

A nature bath sits in between – a man-made pool that uses geothermal heat but with a simpler setting, often out in the countryside. The Secret Lagoon in Fludir and the Myvatn Nature Baths sit near this category, though Myvatn is really a full geothermal spa.

A wild spring or natural pool is free hot water out in nature: a hot river you wade into, a stone-lined pool at the bottom of a valley, or a warm stream at a glacier's edge. There is no supervision, no shower and no guaranteed temperature. This is the purest experience but also the one that demands the most care.

The premium lagoons – what they have in common

Over the past twenty years a whole generation of designed geothermal baths has risen across the country, and today there is hardly a region that does not offer its own lagoon with a view. Most share the same pattern: you book a time online, arrive, get a wristband, shower (without a swimsuit – more on that later), and then stay as long as you like in the hot water.

In nearly all of these places you can rent a towel and often a swimsuit too if you forgot yours, buy a drink at the in-water bar, and eat afterwards. Many offer packages that include a towel, a drink and access to a ritual or steam room. Prices vary by season and time of day and tend to rise at peak times, so it always pays to check prices and book ahead rather than turning up unannounced.

Below we cover the main lagoons and geothermal baths, where they are, what makes each special, and practical details worth knowing. There are many of them and they differ; the right choice depends on where you are in the country and what kind of mood you are after.

The Blue Lagoon – the country's most famous

Blaa lonid (the Blue Lagoon) on the Reykjanes peninsula, between Keflavik airport and Reykjavik, is Iceland's best-known geothermal bath and often the first or last thing visitors experience, since it is so close to the airport. The water is milky blue and rich in silica, algae and minerals, and the white silica mud that people spread on their faces has become one of the most recognisable symbols of Icelandic tourism.

What makes the Blue Lagoon special is the scale, the setting and the contrast: a vast pale-blue expanse of water set in jet-black lava. On site there is a silica mud mask, an in-water bar, steam rooms, restaurants and a luxury hotel. It is a polished but busy experience – you are rarely alone.

Practical notes: booking ahead is all but essential, especially in summer and in the evenings, as the lagoon regularly sells out. Towels and swimsuits can be rented. A good tip for anyone with long hair: apply plenty of conditioner and keep it out of the water, because the silica dries hair and can leave it stiff for a few days.

One more thing: the Reykjanes peninsula has been volcanically active in recent years, and eruptions near Grindavik have at times affected opening and access. It is worth checking the situation on the day if volcanic activity is in the news.

Sky Lagoon – the ocean at the city's edge

Sky Lagoon in Kopavogur, just south of Reykjavik, opened in 2021 and has become one of the most popular baths in the capital area, not least because of how close it is to the city centre. Its signature feature is an infinity edge where the water appears to flow straight into the Atlantic; on a clear day you gaze out at the horizon and mountains while soaking in the hot water.

Sky Lagoon offers a seven-step bathing ritual (the Skjol ritual) that leads you through hot water, a cold plunge, steam, a dry sauna and more – conceived as a cleansing, restorative journey. The setting is modern, with turf walls that nod to old Icelandic building traditions.

Practical notes: book ahead, especially around sunset, which is the most popular time. Towels and facilities are on site, and you can choose between admission options with or without the ritual. Sky Lagoon suits people with little time who still want the lagoon experience, because you can pop over without driving far out of the city.

North and East: Myvatn, Forest Lagoon, GeoSea and Vok

North and East Iceland have their own lagoons, and many are quieter and less crowded than those in the southwest – which many count as a plus.

Jardbodin vid Myvatn (the Myvatn Nature Baths) are often called the Blue Lagoon of the North. They stand on Jardbadsholar with a view over the Myvatn area and offer the same milky-blue, mineral-rich water and steam baths that draw geothermal heat straight from the ground. It is a perfect end to a day at Myvatn, Dettifoss or Krafla.

Skogarbodin (Forest Lagoon) just outside Akureyri opened in 2022 and is unusual in that it lies inside a forest in Vadlaskogur with a view over Eyjafjordur. There is a special feeling to soaking in hot water surrounded by trees – a rare sight in Iceland, where forests are few.

GeoSea in Husavik is a sea bath: geothermally heated seawater is fed into pools on a clifftop overlooking Skjalfandi bay, where whales often play. Husavik is the whale-watching capital, and it is remarkable to spot a spout out on the bay from a warm pool.

Vok Baths near Egilsstadir sit on Lake Urridavatn and are known for floating pools set out in the lake itself. The water there is said to be clean enough to drink. It is a tranquil, refined spot in the calm of the East.

West and the Golden Circle: Krauma, Fontana, Hvammsvik and Secret Lagoon

Near Reykjavik and on the Golden Circle there are numerous baths easy to fold into a day trip.

Krauma stands at Deildartunguhver in Borgarfjordur, the most powerful hot spring in Europe, which gushes an enormous volume of boiling water. At Krauma this hot water is mixed with cold water from a nearby river to reach the perfect bathing temperature, so you are literally sitting in water straight from one of the continent's mightiest springs.

Laugarvatn Fontana on the Golden Circle sits by the lake of the same name and is built over natural hot springs. Here there are steam baths that use the geothermal heat directly, hot tubs and the option to plunge into the cold lake at Laugarvatn. On site they also bake hverabraud in the ground – a rye bread that bakes slowly in the geothermal heat.

Hvammsvik Hot Springs in Hvalfjordur opened in 2022 and are a special place: the pools sit at sea level and the tides affect some of them, so the experience changes with high and low tide. The surroundings in Hvalfjordur are calm and beautiful, only about an hour from the city.

The Secret Lagoon (Gamla laugin) in Fludir is Iceland's oldest swimming pool, built in 1891, and has kept a natural, rustic charm. The water is warm and the backdrop is a small geothermal field with steaming springs. It is not the most polished lagoon, but it has a history and soul that the newer ones sometimes lack.

Blue Lagoon or Sky Lagoon – an honest comparison

This is the most common question, since most people only have time for one premium lagoon near Reykjavik. Here is an honest answer.

Location: Sky Lagoon is in Kopavogur, minutes from central Reykjavik, and is perfect if you have no car or would rather not drive. The Blue Lagoon is on Reykjanes, about 45 minutes from the city but only around 20 minutes from Keflavik airport, which makes it an ideal first or last stop on a trip.

Atmosphere: the Blue Lagoon is larger, more famous and busier, with milky-blue silica water and the contrast against black lava. Sky Lagoon is more compact, focused on the infinity view over the ocean and the seven-step ritual. The Blue Lagoon is about the water itself; Sky Lagoon is about the view and the process.

Price: both sit at the higher end and prices vary, but neither is cheap. If price matters, a public swimming pool is many times more affordable.

Verdict: choose Sky Lagoon if you want a sunset view over the ocean, have no car or little time. Choose the Blue Lagoon if you want to experience the country's most famous lagoon, are arriving or leaving through the airport, or are drawn to the milky-blue silica water. Both are well worth it – neither is a rip-off.

The public pools – the cheapest and most authentic experience

If you want to know Iceland's real bathing culture, skip the lagoon one day and go to the local swimming pool instead. This is the cheapest, most local and often most enjoyable bathing experience there is, and it is everywhere: almost every village, however small, has its own pool with hot tubs.

The hot tub (heitir pottar) is Iceland's social hub. People sit together, discuss the weather, the politics and their neighbours, and visitors are welcome to join. You will learn more about everyday Icelandic life in one hot tub than on many organised tours.

In Reykjavik there are many excellent pools with different characters, and out in the countryside a trip to the pool after a long day of driving can be the highlight of the day. The entry fee is low and you can often rent a towel if you forgot yours.

One important rule applies everywhere: you must shower without a swimsuit and wash thoroughly with soap before entering the pool. This is not optional but a strict requirement, because Icelandic pools use little chlorine and rely on guests arriving clean. More on this in the etiquette section.

Natural pools and wild springs – free, but they demand respect

For many the highlight is not a designed lagoon but a hot pool out in nature, free and under the open sky. Iceland has many such places, but they require caution, respect and good manners.

Reykjadalur above Hveragerdi is the best known: you hike about an hour up the valley and bathe in a hot river where hot and cold water mix, so you can find yourself a comfortable spot. There are boardwalks and simple partitions, but no supervision.

Seljavallalaug under Eyjafjoll is a hidden, historic pool from 1923, one of the oldest in the country, in a beautiful valley after a short walk. The water is lukewarm rather than hot and the pool is not kept very clean, but the setting is unforgettable.

Landmannalaugar in the highlands offers a warm stream beside colourful rhyolite mountains – a classic end to the Fjallabak hiking trails. Hrunalaug near Fludir is a small, charming stone pool on private land; it holds only a few people and the owners have had to endure overuse, so be especially considerate.

Other well-known spots include Grettislaug on Reykjastrond in Skagafjordur, with its link to the Grettis saga and a sea view; Hveravellir on the Kjolur route, a geothermal field with a bathing pool between the Langjokull and Hofsjokull glaciers; and Landbrotalaug on the Snaefellsnes peninsula, a tiny hot pot that holds only two.

The golden rule at all these places: leave nothing but footprints. Take all rubbish with you, never use soap or shampoo in natural pools, respect private land and close gates behind you, and remember that many of these pools are fragile and easily overwhelmed. If a spot is too crowded, come back at another time rather than crowding in.

Etiquette and rules – what everyone must know

Icelandic bathing culture rests on one non-negotiable rule: you shower without a swimsuit and wash thoroughly with soap, all over your body, before entering the pool or lagoon. This is not a suggestion but an obligation, and staff keep an eye out. The reason is simple: Icelandic pools use little or no chlorine and keep the water clean by relying on everyone arriving clean. Showering in a swimsuit or skipping it is considered rude and unhygienic.

Most changing rooms have signs marking the body parts that must be washed – this is not meant to embarrass you, it is simply the norm. Icelanders have done this since childhood and no one is looking at you.

More good habits: tie up long hair, especially in silica-rich water like the Blue Lagoon and Myvatn; apply conditioner before such baths to protect it. No glass is allowed in bathing areas for safety reasons. Talk at a moderate volume, respect others' personal space, and do not photograph strangers. In hot tubs it is fine to chat with locals – it is part of the experience – but read the mood.

In lagoons with in-water bars, your wristband is the payment method; just make sure to drink water regularly, because the hot water dehydrates you more than you think.

What to bring, when to go and how to book

The basic kit is simple: a swimsuit, a towel and flip-flops or bathing shoes. Towels and often swimsuits can be rented at most lagoons and some pools, but it is cheaper to bring your own. Bring a water bottle too, or drink plenty, because dehydration in hot water is more common than people think.

If you wear glasses, know that silica water can leave a film; carry a cloth to clean them. Remove jewellery, especially silver, because geothermal water and sulphur can blacken it. Do not worry about your hair if you remember the conditioner.

Best time: the baths are open and wonderful all year. In summer you enjoy bright evenings and mild days. In winter the experience is a magic of another kind – soaking in scalding water while snowflakes fall, or even watching the northern lights dance over the lagoon if the sky is clear. Sunset is the most popular slot at most lagoons, so book it early.

Booking: the most popular lagoons – the Blue Lagoon and Sky Lagoon in particular – regularly sell out, especially at peak times and around sunset. Book a few days ahead if you can. Public pools and many natural pools require no booking; you simply turn up.

Safety – hot water is not always harmless

Geothermal water can be extremely dangerous. Some water rising straight from the ground is near boiling point and can cause severe burns in an instant. This is especially true of wild springs and natural pools that no one supervises.

The golden rule out in nature: never get into an unmarked spring or natural pool without testing the temperature first with your hand. If the water is too hot to hold your hand in, it is too hot to bathe in. Conditions can change from day to day – flow, temperature and water level in natural pools fluctuate, so a pool that was pleasant last week may be too hot or too cold now.

Do not submerge your head in warm natural pools; this is generally advised against because of rare but serious microbes that can thrive in warm, still water. In designed, supervised lagoons and pools this is not a concern.

Keep an eye on children at all times – hot water tires you and can cause dizziness, and deep pools are dangerous. Take care on slippery, wet stones and rock around natural pools. And if you head to Reykjadalur or another hike to a hot river, dress for the weather; it can be cold and wet on the way even though the water is hot.

Frequently asked questions

Do I need to book the lagoons in advance?

The most popular lagoons, especially the Blue Lagoon and Sky Lagoon, regularly sell out, so booking ahead is strongly recommended, ideally a few days in advance and especially around sunset and in summer. Public swimming pools and most natural pools require no booking.

Why do I have to shower without a swimsuit before entering the pool?

Icelandic pools use little chlorine and rely on guests arriving clean. So it is mandatory to wash thoroughly with soap, without a swimsuit, before getting in. This is a strict rule everywhere and staff keep watch; it is considered completely normal in Iceland and no one is looking at you.

Which is better, the Blue Lagoon or Sky Lagoon?

Both are excellent. Choose Sky Lagoon for the infinity view over the ocean, its closeness to the city centre and the seven-step ritual. Choose the Blue Lagoon for the country's most famous lagoon, its milky-blue silica water and its handy location near the airport. Both sit at the higher price end.

What is the cheapest way to experience Icelandic bathing culture?

The public swimming pool. Almost every town has one with hot tubs, the entry fee is low and the experience is the authentic, local one. The hot tub is a social hub where you meet locals better than on any organised tour.

Are the natural pools free and safe?

Most wild natural pools are free, but they are unsupervised. Always test the temperature with your hand before getting in, because some geothermal water is near boiling point. Conditions change from day to day, so take care, respect private land, and leave nothing but footprints.

What should I bring to the lagoon?

A swimsuit, a towel and flip-flops. Towels and often swimsuits can be rented on site, but bringing your own is cheaper. Also bring conditioner if you visit silica-rich water like the Blue Lagoon, and remove silver jewellery, because sulphur can blacken it.

Can I visit a lagoon in winter?

Yes, and many consider winter the best time. Soaking in scalding water while snowflakes fall, or seeing the northern lights over the lagoon, is unforgettable. The baths are open all year; just dress warmly for the walk to and from the pool.

Is it okay to put my head under water in natural pools?

It is generally advised not to submerge your head in warm, still natural pools because of rare but serious microbes that can thrive in such water. In designed, supervised lagoons and swimming pools this is not a concern.

How do I find pools near me?
