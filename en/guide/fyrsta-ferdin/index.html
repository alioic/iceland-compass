Your First Trip to Iceland: Practical Tips You Should Know

Everything a first-time visitor needs: how to get around, pay, dress, and enjoy Iceland's powerful nature safely and respectfully.

Getting There and Your First Steps

Almost everyone who visits Iceland arrives at Keflavík Airport, the country's main international gateway. It sits on the Reykjanes peninsula, roughly a 45-minute drive from Reykjavík. This surprises many people: the capital is not right at the airport, so you will need to travel a short distance to reach it.

From the airport you have several options. You can take an airport coach or shuttle bus directly to Reykjavík, book a taxi, or pick up a rental car right at the terminal. Many travelers choose the last option because it gives them the freedom to explore on their own from the very start.

A good rule is to plan your first night in advance and know exactly how you will get from the airport to your accommodation. After a long flight, it is a relief not to have to figure that out on the spot. If you land late at night, remember that some services may be limited at that hour, so arrange your transfer before you set off.

Getting Around the Country

Iceland is large and sparsely populated, and its most beautiful places are often far from towns. Renting a car is the most flexible way to see the country, because you control the pace, the stops, and the route. The Ring Road circles the island and connects many of its best-known destinations.

If you would rather not drive, there are good alternatives. Organized day tours run from Reykjavík to popular spots like the Golden Circle and the south coast, and a bus network links the larger towns. These options are convenient, but they offer less freedom than having your own vehicle.

Keep in mind that distances are greater than the map suggests, and conditions can change quickly. In winter, roads may close due to weather, and some highland routes are open only in summer and require a four-wheel-drive vehicle. Always check road conditions and the forecast before any longer drive, and allow yourself plenty of time for each leg of the journey.

Money, Water, and Daily Life

Iceland is very much a card-based society. Payment cards are accepted almost everywhere, even for small amounts like a cup of coffee or a bus ticket. You need very little cash, and many visitors use none at all during their whole trip. Just make sure your card works abroad and that you know your PIN.

It is worth saying plainly that Iceland is generally an expensive country for visitors, especially food, drink, and accommodation. It pays to budget for this and to use grocery stores for some of your meals rather than eating out every time.

One thing clearly saves money: the tap water. Icelandic tap water is clean, cold, and excellent to drink, and it is free. There is no need to buy bottled water. Bring a reusable bottle and simply refill it. If the hot water smells faintly of sulphur, that is normal and harmless; the cold water is odorless and perfect for drinking.

Swimming-Pool Culture and Shower Etiquette

Swimming pools are at the heart of everyday Icelandic life. Almost every town has one, often with hot tubs, and people go there to relax, meet neighbors, and unwind. For a visitor, a local pool is an inexpensive and genuine way to experience the culture.

There is one custom you must know in advance: you are expected to shower and wash thoroughly without a swimsuit before entering the pool. This is not optional but a basic rule of hygiene, because the pools use very little chlorine. Soap up everywhere, including the areas that signs or diagrams point out, rinse well, and only then put on your swimsuit.

This can feel unfamiliar the first few times, but locals consider it completely normal and genuinely appreciate visitors who respect it. Changing rooms are separated by gender, and staff are usually happy to help if you are unsure. Once you are settled into the warm water under an open sky, you will understand exactly why Icelanders love their pools.

Weather, Nature, and Safety

Icelandic weather is famous for changing fast. You can get sun, rain, and strong wind on the same day, whatever the season. The key is to dress in layers: a base layer that keeps you warm, a warm mid-layer, and a windproof and waterproof outer shell. Good walking shoes and a hat matter a great deal, even in summer.

Always check the forecast before you head out. The website vedur.is provides weather forecasts and warnings, and safetravel.is offers information on travel safety and conditions at your destinations. It takes only a few minutes and can change your day for the better.

Nature here is powerful and should be enjoyed with respect. Stay on marked paths and trails, because the vegetation and moss are fragile and slow to recover. Off-road driving is illegal and leaves scars on the land that remain visible for years. Close gates behind you where there is livestock, keep your distance from cliff edges, waves along the shore, and glaciers, and obey closures and warnings. Do not push beyond your own ability; safety first, the view can wait.

Practical Details and a First-Timer Checklist

A few more points make the trip smoother. Alcohol is sold only in Vínbúðin, the state liquor stores, and in licensed restaurants and bars; you will not find it in ordinary supermarkets. Tipping is not expected in Iceland, as service is included, though nobody will stop you from leaving a little something if you wish.

English is widely spoken and most Icelanders speak it well, so you will get by easily as an English-speaking visitor. Mobile coverage is good across most inhabited areas, and it is simple to buy an Icelandic SIM card or use an eSIM to stay connected while you travel.

A short checklist for first-timers: windproof and waterproof clothing with layers underneath; good walking shoes; a reusable water bottle; a payment card that works abroad; swimwear and a towel; a SIM or eSIM; a booked airport transfer; and the sites vedur.is and safetravel.is saved on your phone. With these in hand, you are well prepared.

Welcome to Iceland. If you come with an open mind, dress for the weather, and treat the nature with respect, your first trip is likely to be the first of many.

Frequently asked questions

Do I need to rent a car, or can I manage without one?

You can manage without a car if you stay mainly in Reykjavík and join organized day tours. But to see the country at your own pace and reach more remote places, a rental car is the most flexible option.

Do I need to bring cash?

Almost none. Iceland is a card-based society and payment cards are accepted nearly everywhere, even for small amounts. Just make sure your card works abroad before you travel.

Why do I have to shower without a swimsuit before swimming?

Icelandic pools use very little chlorine and rely instead on everyone washing thoroughly beforehand. It is a basic rule of hygiene, and locals genuinely appreciate visitors who respect it.

Is the tap water safe to drink?

Yes, completely. The cold tap water is clean, cold, and excellent to drink, and it is free. There is no need to buy bottled water; just bring a reusable bottle and refill it.
