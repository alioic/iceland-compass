Iceland in 5 to 7 Days: Ready-Made Self-Drive Itineraries

Realistic day-by-day plans for a self-driving visitor: a five-day South and West trip or a seven-day Ring Road loop, with driving times and overnight bases.

How to Think About an Iceland Trip

The best way to experience Iceland is behind the wheel of your own car. You set the pace, stop where you like and reach places that bus tours skip. Public transport between destinations out in the countryside is limited, so a rental car gives you a freedom nothing else matches.

Route 1, usually called the Ring Road, forms a complete loop around the island and is about 1,320 km long. It links most of the best-known destinations and is paved almost the whole way. You can either focus on the southwest corner in five days, or drive the full loop in seven to ten days.

Let us be honest from the start: in five to seven days you cannot see everything. Iceland is larger than people expect and the distances are longer. If you try to cram it all in, you will spend the trip in the car instead of enjoying the places. That is why it is better to choose one of two approaches: a compact South and West trip that lets you breathe, or a full loop that is an adventure but also a good deal more driving.

Below you get both itineraries day by day, with realistic stops, hedged driving distances and a suggested overnight base for each night. The figures are approximate; weather, roads and how often you stop will always shift them a little.

One tip before we go on: treat the first and last days as half days. You often land tired after a flight, need to pick up the rental car and get used to the traffic, and on the last day you have to return the car and reach the airport in good time. Do not put big driving distances on those two days. If your flight lands late at night or leaves early in the morning, it can pay to spend the first and last night near Keflavík rather than in Reykjavík.

The Five-Day South and West Trip: Who It Suits

This plan is ideal for first-time visitors, for anyone with only a few days, and for winter travel. You do not drive the full loop but instead focus on the most concentrated and accessible part of the country: the capital, the Golden Circle, the South Coast and an optional day on Reykjanes, Snæfellsnes or in Reykjavík.

The advantages are real. Driving distances are shorter, you are never far from services, and you can keep the same accommodation for two or three nights instead of packing up every morning. This is also the safest option in winter, because you stay on well-travelled roads where maintenance is best.

You will miss the East Fjords, Mývatn and the North, but in return you get time to enjoy what you do see. For many first trips that is exactly the right call. Total driving across all five days is roughly 700 to 900 km depending on how you spend the fifth day.

Another advantage is how flexible this route is. If the weather turns bad on one day you can swap days around, take a bath day or a museum day in Reykjavík and save the South Coast until it clears. Because you are never far from the capital, a plan B is always within reach, which is invaluable when the Icelandic weather shows its moody side.

Five-Day Itinerary: Day by Day

Day 1 – Reykjavík and the Blue Lagoon or Sky Lagoon. Land, pick up the car and take the day slowly. Walk the downtown, see Hallgrímskirkja and Harpa, and end the day in a warm bath. The Blue Lagoon is about a 20-minute drive from Keflavík Airport and works well right on arrival or departure; Sky Lagoon is under 15 minutes from downtown with an ocean view. Book your time slot in advance, as both lagoons fill up. Overnight: Reykjavík.

Day 2 – The Golden Circle. The classic day trip: Þingvellir, where world-famous geology and the meeting of tectonic plates come together, Geysir and Strokkur which erupts every few minutes, and Gullfoss in full force. The drive from Reykjavík and back around is about 230 to 250 km, comfortably done in a day. If you want to add a soak, the Secret Lagoon at Flúðir is a natural and relaxed addition. Overnight: Reykjavík, or in the South near Selfoss or Hella if you want to shorten the next morning.

Day 3 – The South Coast. Now the most dramatic stretch begins. Seljalandsfoss, which you can walk behind, and the hidden Gljúfrabúi right beside it; Skógafoss, broad and powerful; Reynisfjara with its black sands, basalt columns and dangerous sneaker waves that you must never underestimate; and finally the village of Vík í Mýrdal. From Reykjavík to Vík is about 190 km. Overnight: Vík or nearby.

Day 4 – Jökulsárlón and Diamond Beach. This is the longest day. From Vík east to Jökulsárlón is about another 190 km, where icebergs float on the lagoon and wash up on the black Diamond Beach across the road. Along the way you can stop at Fjaðrárgljúfur canyon and look in at Skaftafell in Vatnajökull National Park. You can either drive back to Vík the same day, a long but doable stretch, or spend a night closer, for example near Kirkjubæjarklaustur, to shorten the day. Overnight: Vík or Kirkjubæjarklaustur.

Day 5 – Reykjanes, Snæfellsnes or Reykjavík. On the way back you have a choice. The Reykjanes peninsula is a short loop of geothermal areas, lava fields and lighthouses, well suited to your last day before departure. If you have more time and energy, Snæfellsnes is a longer but magnificent loop with Kirkjufell, the Snæfellsjökull glacier and fishing villages. If you would rather slow down, simply end in Reykjavík with museums, restaurants and shopping. Overnight: Reykjavík.

The Seven-Day Ring Road Loop: Who It Suits

A seven-day loop around the Ring Road is the big adventure. You see the whole country: the South Coast, the East Fjords, Mývatn, Akureyri and the North, and finally the west on the way home. It suits those who want to experience the variety and are happy to be on the move every day.

Be aware, though: seven days for the full loop is a brisk pace. Some days involve three to five hours behind the wheel, and you have limited time at each place. This works best in summer, when daylight lasts nearly around the clock and roads are open. If you have eight to ten days, the same route becomes far more relaxed, and we strongly recommend it if you can manage it.

You can drive the loop clockwise (south first) or anti-clockwise (west and north first). Most people go clockwise because the trip then starts with the most spectacular part, the South Coast, while you are still freshest. The itinerary below is clockwise. Total driving is about 1,320 km plus detours, often closer to 1,500 km over the week.

There is one more argument for going clockwise: if the weather is poor on the first day along the South Coast you can still adjust, but if you save the most exposed, weather-dependent places until last, you have no spare days left. So start with what you most want to see and do not count on the final days to rescue everything.

Seven-Day Itinerary: Day by Day

Day 1 – Reykjavík east along the South Coast to Vík. Set off early and take the gems of the South Coast: Seljalandsfoss, Skógafoss and Reynisfjara, and stay in Vík. About 190 km, but with many stops it fills a whole day. If you like, you can slot the Golden Circle in before starting the loop, but then you need an eighth day. Overnight: Vík.

Day 2 – Vík east to Höfn via Jökulsárlón. Today you pass Fjaðrárgljúfur canyon, Skaftafell and on to Jökulsárlón and Diamond Beach, ending in the town of Höfn í Hornafirði, known for its langoustine. About 270 km. This is one of the most beautiful days of the trip, with glaciers on one side almost the whole way. Overnight: Höfn or nearby.

Day 3 – Höfn through the East Fjords to Egilsstaðir. The route winds around fjords and over heaths with pretty seaside villages such as Djúpivogur and Fáskrúðsfjörður. You can take the coast or the shorter route over Öxi (a mountain road that can close in bad weather). About 250 to 270 km. Overnight: Egilsstaðir, or Seyðisfjörður, a scenic detour over the mountain.

Day 4 – Egilsstaðir west to Mývatn. Today you reach one of the country's most extraordinary geothermal areas. Along the way is Dettifoss, the most powerful waterfall in Europe, and around Mývatn are craters, hot springs and the Nature Baths, the North's answer to the Blue Lagoon. About 165 km to Mývatn, though Dettifoss requires a detour. Overnight: Mývatn or Reykjahlíð.

Day 5 – Mývatn west via Goðafoss to Akureyri and the North. Stop at Goðafoss, one of the country's loveliest waterfalls, on the way to Akureyri, the capital of the North, with cafés, a botanical garden and good food. About 100 km, so you have time to enjoy it. If you have energy, whale watching from Húsavík is unforgettable. Overnight: Akureyri.

Day 6 – Akureyri south and west to Snæfellsnes or Borgarfjörður. This is the longest driving day. From Akureyri south through Norðurárdalur and toward the west it is about 250 to 390 km depending on whether you stop in Borgarnes or push all the way out onto Snæfellsnes. Snæfellsnes is magnificent but adds considerable driving; Borgarfjörður, with Hraunfossar and the Deildartunguhver hot spring, is a shorter and calmer option. Overnight: Snæfellsnes, Borgarnes or nearby.

Day 7 – The West back to Reykjavík. The final day is short. From Borgarnes to Reykjavík is only about 75 km through the Hvalfjörður tunnel, so you can take it easy, stop along the way and be back in town with time to return the car. If you stayed on Snæfellsnes, you finish the loop on the way south. Overnight: Reykjavík.

Summer Versus Winter

The season changes everything about what is realistic. In summer, from June into August, it is bright almost around the clock. You can drive longer, linger longer and enjoy a flexibility that winter travel does not allow. Almost all roads are open, including the highland roads in July and August, though those are not part of these itineraries.

In winter, from November into March, the days shorten dramatically and the weather can turn in an instant. Then we recommend shortening the plans, staying in the South and West and building buffer days into your schedule for weather. The full Ring Road in winter is riskier; impassable roads, ice and closures can easily disrupt a schedule, and some mountain roads close entirely.

That does not mean winter is a bad idea, quite the opposite. Northern lights, ice caves and quiet make it magical. But keep the plan shorter, the car sturdy and your mindset flexible. Reckon that one day might go to waiting out the weather, and plan so that it does not matter if it does.

How Much to Drive Per Day

The most common mistake visitors make is overestimating how much ground they can cover. On a map 300 km looks harmless, but once you add stops, photos, meals and detours the day grows long.

A good rule of thumb is to aim for a few hours of actual driving per day, not a whole day behind the wheel. On the five-day South trip most days are under 200 km, which gives you plenty of time to genuinely stop. On the seven-day loop some days reach 250 to 390 km, and you feel the difference: less time at each place, more tiredness by evening.

Always leave a margin. Roads in Iceland are often single-lane, with single-lane bridges, loose gravel and livestock that may wander onto the road. You drive slower than you think. If your plan assumes you drive from morning to night, you probably have too much scheduled; take one stop out rather than rush past everything.

Where to Stay and Booking Ahead

In summer, especially from mid-June into mid-August, rural accommodation fills up early. In smaller towns such as Vík, in Höfn and around Mývatn supply is limited, and the best options sell out many weeks, even months, in advance. So book as soon as your dates are set.

On the five-day South trip you can simplify your life by staying two nights in the same place, for example in Reykjavík for the first half and in or near Vík for the second. That saves packing and brings calm. On the seven-day loop, by contrast, you change accommodation nearly every night, so planning matters even more; know in advance where you sleep each night.

Out in the countryside the choices are a mix of guesthouses, country hotels, farm stays and campsites. We do not name specific hotels here, since availability changes, but look for accommodation in or near the towns named in the itineraries; then you are well placed for the next day.

Keep in mind, too, that out in the countryside the choice of restaurants is limited and many close early, especially outside peak season. It pays to eat when you get the chance rather than count on finding an open place when you arrive in the evening. In some spots the biggest petrol station is also the only diner, and in smaller towns it can be wise to carry supplies for lunch. Breakfast is usually included with rural accommodation, which is handy when you want to set off early.

The Car, Fuel and Roads

For both itineraries in summer an ordinary passenger car is perfectly adequate; the Ring Road and every place named here is on pavement or good roads. A four-wheel-drive is only necessary for highland roads, the so-called F-roads, which are not part of these trips. In winter we still recommend a larger car with good winter tyres, since conditions can be snowy.

Fuel is not available everywhere in the countryside, so fill the tank more often than you think you need to. On quieter stretches, for example in the East Fjords or on the way to Mývatn, there can be long distances between petrol stations. A rule that pays off: fill up when the tank is half empty, not when it is empty.

Before each leg, check road.is for road conditions and closures and vedur.is for the weather forecast. These two sites are invaluable, especially outside summer. Always respect closures and warnings; they are not suggestions but are set for safety. And remember the fundamental rule: off-road driving is strictly forbidden in Iceland and scars fragile nature that takes decades or centuries to recover. Stay on marked roads and tracks.

One thing that surprises many people is how strong the wind can be. In a gale you need to take care, keep both hands on the wheel and pay special attention to car doors, because the wind can rip them open and damage them in an instant; that kind of damage is rarely covered by insurance. On exposed stretches, for example near Vík or on the sands to its east, the wind can be so strong that it is sensible to wait out the worst gusts. When the Met Office issues orange or red warnings, it is simply best to postpone driving that day.

What to Skip and How Not to Over-Schedule

The temptation is always the same: to try to see everything. But Iceland rewards those who pick fewer places and enjoy them well. It is better to stand for ten minutes and watch Gullfoss in peace than to rush past it to reach the next point on a list.

The highlands are the biggest thing to skip. Landmannalaugar and Þórsmörk are magnificent places, but they require a four-wheel-drive, driving across unbridged rivers and more time than a five to seven-day trip allows without sacrificing something else. Save them for a longer or dedicated trip.

On the five-day trip, do not try to add the North; that means several extra hours of driving that eat up the whole trip. On the seven-day loop, resist the temptation to take a detour every day. Choose the one or two you want most and skip the rest. A well-planned trip with breathing room always beats one that is packed but exhausting.

Summary: Which Itinerary Is for You

Choose the five-day South and West trip if this is your first visit, if you are travelling in winter, if you want shorter driving days, or if you value being able to stay in the same place for more than one night. You see the most spectacular and accessible part of the country without being constantly on the move.

Choose the seven-day loop if you want to see the whole country, are travelling in summer and are content to be on the road every day with longer driving stretches. You experience the variety, from the South Coast to the East Fjords and the North, but you pay for it in time behind the wheel.

And if you have the option of eight to ten days, take the loop at a gentler pace. The same places, more breathing room, less fatigue. Whatever you choose: book accommodation early, keep an eye on road.is and vedur.is, drive carefully and leave time to stop. It is in the stops, not in the driving, that Iceland becomes unforgettable.

Frequently asked questions

Is five days enough for Iceland?

Yes, five days is plenty to properly experience the southwest corner: Reykjavík, the Golden Circle, the South Coast and Jökulsárlón, with an optional day on Reykjanes or Snæfellsnes. You will not drive the full loop, but you see the most spectacular places without rushing. For a first trip this is often the best length.

Can you drive the whole Ring Road in seven days?

Yes, it is doable but it is a brisk pace. The loop is about 1,320 km and with detours often closer to 1,500 km, so some days involve three to five hours behind the wheel. It works best in summer when daylight is long and roads are open. If you have eight to ten days, the same route becomes much more relaxed.

Should I drive the loop clockwise or anti-clockwise?

Most people go clockwise, that is south first, because the trip then starts with the most spectacular part, the South Coast, while you are still freshest. Both directions work equally well in terms of distance, so it is a matter of taste. Anti-clockwise can be nice if you want to save the South Coast for last.

Do I need a four-wheel-drive?

Not for these itineraries in summer. The Ring Road and every place named here is on pavement or good roads, and an ordinary passenger car is enough. A four-wheel-drive is only necessary for highland roads, the so-called F-roads, which are not part of these trips. In winter it is wise to choose a larger car with good winter tyres.

How far should I drive per day?

Aim for a few hours of actual driving per day rather than a whole day behind the wheel. On the five-day South trip most days are under 200 km; on the seven-day loop some days reach 250 to 390 km. Always leave time to stop, because that is where the trip becomes memorable.

When do I need to book accommodation?

As early as you can, especially in summer. From mid-June into mid-August accommodation in smaller towns such as Vík, in Höfn and around Mývatn fills up many weeks ahead. As soon as your dates are set you should book, otherwise you may have to drive farther than you wanted to find a room.

Is it safe to do these trips in winter?

The five-day South trip is realistic in winter if you shorten it, stay on well-travelled roads and allow buffer days for weather. The full Ring Road in winter is riskier because of impassable roads, ice and closures, and we recommend the South and West instead. Always check road.is and vedur.is and respect all warnings.

Can I reach Landmannalaugar or Þórsmörk on this kind of trip?

Not easily. Both areas are in the highlands, require a four-wheel-drive and driving across unbridged rivers, and open only in summer. They also take a lot of time. On a five to seven-day trip you would have to sacrifice something else to reach them, so we recommend saving them for a longer or dedicated trip.

Should I bathe in the Blue Lagoon or Sky Lagoon?

Both are excellent; the choice comes down to location. The Blue Lagoon is about 20 minutes from Keflavík Airport and works well right on arrival or just before departure. Sky Lagoon is under 15 minutes from downtown Reykjavík with an ocean view and suits the first evening. Book your time slot in advance in either case, as they fill up.
