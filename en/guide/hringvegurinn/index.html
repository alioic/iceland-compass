Driving the Ring Road (Route 1): A Practical Guide

Everything you need to drive Iceland's Ring Road: timing, direction, renting a car, key highlights, driving conditions and safety.

What the Ring Road actually is

The Ring Road, officially Route 1, is the national highway that loops all the way around Iceland, roughly 1,322 kilometres in total. It is paved for nearly its entire length and links most of the country's regions together: the South Coast, the East Fjords, the North, and back to Reykjavik through the West. For many travellers it is the simplest way to experience Iceland's variety in one continuous journey without venturing into the highlands.

The road itself is comfortable to drive in summer, but much of it is a single lane in each direction and it winds through mountains, fjords and coastlines. That means your average speed is lower than you might expect. You are not driving a motorway; you are following a route that hugs the landscape, and that is precisely why the trip is so rewarding.

Keep in mind that the Ring Road is the backbone, but many of the most memorable places sit on side roads branching off it. Snaefellsnes, the Westfjords, the Myvatn area and several waterfall routes require a short detour. It helps to think of the loop as the spine and the highlights as branches reaching outward from it.

How long to allow

The honest answer is to give yourself 7 to 10 days if you want to enjoy the drive at a relaxed pace. That leaves time to stop at waterfalls, walk short trails, eat without rushing and react to the weather without stress. Ten days or more opens up side trips such as Snaefellsnes or longer hikes along the way.

The practical minimum is around 5 days if you are focused and willing to drive a fair amount each day. It is doable, but the trip becomes brisk and you will miss a lot. Trying to complete the loop in 3 days is not recommended: you end up spending nearly all your time behind the wheel instead of actually experiencing the country.

A good rule of thumb is to average no more than 3 to 4 hours of driving per day and always leave a buffer. Distances look short on a map, but weather, stops and gravel stretches all add time. It is far better to see fewer places properly than many in a hurry.

Direction, planning and renting a car

Most people drive the loop counter-clockwise, heading east along the South Coast first. The advantage is that you reach the most dramatic waterfalls and the Jokulsarlon glacier lagoon early, which sets a high bar from day one. Going clockwise works just as well and can spread out the traffic, so simply choose whatever fits your bookings best.

The single most important planning decision is the car. Almost every traveller drives the Ring Road in a rental, because public transport is limited outside towns and the whole appeal of the loop is the freedom to stop wherever you like. I strongly recommend booking a rental car early, especially in summer, since the most popular vehicles and the better rates tend to sell out well in advance.

For the Ring Road itself in summer, a standard two-wheel-drive car is perfectly adequate, as the route is paved almost the entire way. If, however, you plan to take mountain roads (F-roads), venture into the highlands, or travel in winter, you will need a four-wheel-drive (4x4) and ideally some experience with those conditions. Consider your insurance carefully too: sand and ash protection and gravel cover are common add-ons in Iceland. Pick a size that suits your luggage and party, and check that the heating and tyres match the season.

The key regions and highlights

The South Coast is usually the first leg and one of the most popular. It holds the Seljalandsfoss and Skogafoss waterfalls, the black sand beach at Reynisfjara, glacier lagoons and the highlight of Jokulsarlon with Breidamerkursandur (Diamond Beach) close by. Between them, the Vatnajokull ice cap reaches down toward the road in several places.

Further east, the East Fjords take over, with a winding road threading through quiet fjords and small fishing villages, and often fewer tourists. This is a calmer stretch of the loop and a good place to catch your breath. From there the route heads north to Myvatn, where geothermal activity, craters, hot springs and natural bathing pools shape the landscape, and on to Akureyri, the capital of the north.

In the West, on the final stretch of the loop, it is well worth detouring out to the Snaefellsnes peninsula. It is sometimes called 'Iceland in miniature' because of how much variety it packs in: a glacier, beaches, bird cliffs and fishing villages in a relatively small area. This detour adds a day or two to the trip but rewards anyone with the time to spare.

Driving conditions and safety

Icelandic roads make demands that surprise some visitors. In many places you will meet single-lane bridges where only one car crosses at a time; the vehicle nearer the bridge goes first and the other waits. Pavement can turn to gravel without warning, so slow down considerably because loose gravel is slippery. Blind rises and sharp bends are common, so keep a comfortable speed below the limit.

The weather can change quickly, even in summer, and wind is a particular hazard. Open your car doors carefully in strong gusts, because the wind can damage them. Always check conditions at road.is and safety information at safetravel.is before you set off, and leave a travel plan if you head somewhere remote.

In winter the journey becomes a different undertaking altogether. Daylight is short, ice is common and some sections can close temporarily. A winter loop is possible but calls for four-wheel-drive, studded tyres, extra time and flexibility. If you are not used to winter driving in Iceland, consider shorter trips out from Reykjavik instead.

Fuel, practicalities and a sample pace

Fuel stations are spread out and can be far apart on the quieter stretches, particularly in the East Fjords and around the edge of the highlands. A good habit is to refill when the tank drops to half rather than relying on the next station. Many self-service pumps accept cards only, so keep a payment card ready. Food and accommodation are pricier than in many countries, so budget accordingly.

Here is a relaxed 8-day sample itinerary going counter-clockwise. Day 1, Reykjavik to Vik via the South Coast waterfalls. Day 2, Vik to Hofn with a stop at Jokulsarlon. Day 3, the East Fjords. Day 4, eastward on to Myvatn. Day 5, Myvatn and its surroundings. Day 6, Akureyri and the north. Day 7, a detour around Snaefellsnes or the West. Day 8, back to Reykjavik.

This is only a skeleton; adapt it to your interests, the weather and your energy. Leave one 'spare' day if you possibly can, because it often saves the trip when weather or an unexpected discovery upends the plan. The Ring Road is not a race but an invitation to travel at your own pace.

Frequently asked questions

Do I need a 4x4 for the Ring Road?

No, not for the Ring Road itself in summer; a standard two-wheel-drive car is fine because the route is paved almost the entire way. You do need a four-wheel-drive (4x4) if you plan to take F-roads, enter the highlands, or travel in winter.

When is the best time to drive the loop?

June to September is the easiest window: roads are open, daylight is long and conditions are mild. A winter trip is possible and quieter, but it demands a 4x4, experience, extra time and flexibility because of ice, darkness and possible closures.

Can I use public transport instead of renting a car?

Not really, for a proper loop. Bus service is limited outside towns and many of the best places sit on side roads. Almost everyone drives the Ring Road in a rental car, which gives you the freedom to stop and adapt the trip to the weather and your interests.

What is the minimum number of days I need?

About 5 days is a realistic minimum if you drive a fair amount each day, but 7 to 10 days gives a far better experience. More days create room for side trips such as Snaefellsnes and for the weather, which changes often.
