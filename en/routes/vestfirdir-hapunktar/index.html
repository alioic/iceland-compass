Westfjords highlights

Iceland's most untouched corner

The Westfjords are the most remote and one of the most unspoiled parts of Iceland — the steepest fjords, sheer bird cliffs and few tourists. The highlights are the majestic Dynjandi waterfall, the Látrabjarg bird cliff with its tame puffins, the Hornstrandir nature reserve, the regional capital Ísafjörður, the Museum of Sorcery in Hólmavík and natural pools like Reykjafjarðarlaug and Pollurinn. For travellers who want to get off the beaten track.

The Westfjords – Iceland's Wild and Remote Northwest

The Westfjords are the oldest and most remote corner of Iceland, a dramatic region where steep mountains plunge into deep fjords and the roads wind endlessly along the shoreline. Only a small share of Iceland's visitors ever make it out here, and that is precisely the appeal: this is Iceland as it once was – quiet, monumental and largely untouched. In summer the region bursts into life, with millions of seabirds nesting on the cliffs, seals hauled out on the skerries and the small fishing villages at their liveliest. From Ísafjörður, the region's charming capital, you can explore the fjords, the outermost headlands and even the roadless wilderness of the Hornstrandir nature reserve, where nature alone is in charge.

Dynjandi, Látrabjarg and Rauðisandur – Highlights of the Westfjords

Dynjandi is the undisputed queen of Westfjords waterfalls, a roughly hundred-metre series of cascades that fans out like a bridal veil at the head of Arnarfjörður. Further south, Látrabjarg – one of Europe's largest bird cliffs and the westernmost point of Iceland – lets you watch puffins at remarkably close range in summer. Nearby lies Rauðisandur, a vast beach whose sands shift from golden to reddish with the changing light. Ísafjörður charms visitors with its old timber houses and lively cultural scene, and boats from Ísafjörður and Bolungarvík carry hikers to Hornstrandir, an uninhabited reserve famous for its Arctic foxes. The hot pots deserve a chapter of their own: Reykjafjarðarlaug in Arnarfjörður, the seaside tubs at Drangsnes and Pollurinn near Tálknafjörður.

Gravel Roads and a Short Summer – Practical Tips for the Westfjords

The best time to visit the Westfjords is from early summer into autumn; many mountain roads become impassable in winter and much of the tourist infrastructure closes outside the high season. Parts of the road network are still gravel, especially in the southern fjords, so allow generous driving times and take it slow. Hornstrandir can only be reached by boat and has no roads, no shops and unreliable phone signal, so trips there need careful planning. At the natural hot pots, the usual Icelandic rules apply: wash before you soak, treat the sites gently and leave nothing behind. Give the Westfjords at least two to three days – distances here are always longer than they look on the map.

Places on this route

  • Dynjandi The thundering bridal veil of the Westfjords - a roughly 100 m tiered waterfall.
  • Látrabjarg The westernmost tip of Europe and one of the largest bird cliffs in the world.
  • Hornstrandir A pristine, roadless nature reserve where the Arctic fox reigns supreme.
  • Ísafjörður The largest town in the Westfjords, encircled by steep mountains.
  • Hólmavík A fishing village in the Strandir region, known for the Museum of Sorcery and Witchcraft.
  • Reykjafjarðarlaug A peaceful geothermal pool at the head of Reykjafjörður in Arnarfjörður.
  • Pollurinn Beloved hillside hot pots just outside the village of Tálknafjörður.
