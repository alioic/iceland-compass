The Northern Lights in Iceland: a complete guide to when, where and how to see them

Everything you need to know about the aurora in Iceland — the season, weather, reading the forecast, best spots, photography and realistic expectations.

What are the northern lights? The science behind the dancing sky

The northern lights, or aurora borealis, appear when charged particles from the Sun collide with Earth's atmosphere. The Sun constantly releases a stream of charged particles known as the solar wind. When this wind reaches Earth, our planet's magnetic field — the magnetosphere — funnels some of those particles toward the magnetic poles. There they pour down into the upper atmosphere and collide with molecules of oxygen and nitrogen.

Each collision bumps a molecule into a higher-energy state, and as it settles back down it releases that energy as light. That glow is what we see rippling across the sky. Iceland is exceptionally well placed for this because the whole country sits directly beneath the auroral oval, the ring around the magnetic pole where activity is most concentrated.

The colours depend on which gas is involved and at what altitude the collision happens. Green is by far the most common colour, produced by oxygen at roughly 100 to 150 kilometres up — and since our eyes are most sensitive to green, it is the shade that stands out most. Red comes from oxygen at much higher altitudes, often above 200 kilometres, and tends to appear only during strong activity. Purple and blue come from nitrogen and often show up at the lower edges or fringes of the curtains. When everything lines up, the sky can glow in green waves tinged with red and violet.

When can you see the aurora? Season, months and time of night

The aurora itself happens all year round, but you can only see it when the sky is dark enough. That is why the viewing season in Iceland runs roughly from late August to mid-April. Through high summer the midnight sun keeps the sky too bright — even when activity is strong, you see nothing because it never truly gets dark. This is exactly why you cannot see the northern lights in June and July, no matter how lucky you are otherwise.

Many people point to September to October and February to March as especially good months. The reasoning is twofold: there is already plenty of darkness, but the weather is often a little more settled and the cloud cover more forgiving than in the depths of winter, and the temperatures are more bearable while you wait around. Midwinter offers the most darkness but also the most unstable weather. In practice, any dark and clear night in the season can be excellent.

The best time of night is roughly 21:00 to 01:00, when activity often peaks, though truthfully any dark hour of the night can deliver. It pays to be patient and to step outside for a look at regular intervals through midnight and beyond.

It is also worth mentioning the solar cycle, an eleven-year rhythm that governs how active the Sun is. Through the years around the mid-2020s we have been near solar maximum, when activity tends to be higher on average. That does not mean every single night is better — activity varies from year to year and from night to night, and nothing is ever guaranteed.

The three conditions you need — and why clear sky matters most

To see the northern lights, three conditions have to line up at the same time. First, darkness: the sky must be dark, which rules out summer and, to some degree, the brightest nights around a full moon. Second, a clear sky: you need to be able to see the stars. Third, enough solar or geomagnetic activity to switch the lights on in the first place.

Of these three, cloud cover is by far the most common thing that ruins an evening. Iceland is one of the cloudiest countries in Europe, and a thick blanket of cloud will hide the aurora completely, no matter how strong the activity is above it. Visitors often fixate on a high activity number but forget that powerful lights behind solid cloud simply cannot be seen.

This is why a clear sky is, in practice, more important than a high activity number. Moderate activity under a starry sky beats strong activity under cloud every time. It is also why being mobile helps so much: if you can drive toward a break in the clouds, your chances are far better than someone stuck under a single unbroken overcast.

How to read the forecast — the Met Office, cloud maps and the activity scale

The Icelandic Met Office publishes a dedicated aurora forecast at vedur.is, and it is the single best tool you have. The forecast combines two things: a map showing the predicted cloud cover over the country, and a number describing the expected aurora activity.

The cloud map is the crucial part. On it, white or pale areas mean clear sky, while green or dark areas mean cloud, usually split into high, medium and low cloud layers. Your goal is simple: find the white patches you can reach and head for them. The map shows forecasts for different times of the evening, so you can see how the clouds are expected to move.

Activity is given on a scale from 0 to 9. This number is tied to the Kp index, an international measure of geomagnetic activity that also runs from 0 to 9. The higher the number, the stronger and more widespread the lights tend to be, and the further south on the globe they can be seen. What many people do not realise is that in Iceland you do not need a high number. Because the country lies directly beneath the auroral oval, activity of just 2 or 3 can produce a stunning display if the sky is clear.

In short: compare the cloud map against the activity number and add your own location into the mix. Look for a clear patch, give even moderate activity a chance, and get yourself away from light pollution. Those three together matter far more than any single high number.

Where to see them — dark-sky spots and chasing clear weather

You do not need to go far. What matters is getting away from town lights so your eyes adjust to the dark and the fainter glow becomes visible. Even a short drive out of town can change everything.

Among the well-known dark-sky spots near the southwest is Þingvellir, where open space and darkness go hand in hand. The South Coast is full of open areas with little light pollution, and Snæfellsnes is a classic — the mountain Kirkjufell is one of the most photographed aurora foregrounds in the world. In truth, any stretch of countryside anywhere in the country works, as long as the sky is dark and clear. Any of the regions we cover on the site can deliver a good show when conditions are right.

If you want or need to stay in Reykjavík, spots such as Grótta on Seltjarnarnes and the Öskjuhlíð hill have less light pollution than the city centre, though open country will always be better. Put your phone away or set it to a red light, and give your eyes at least fifteen to twenty minutes to adjust to the dark.

The biggest advantage of having a car is flexibility. An aurora night is often about chasing gaps in the cloud, and whoever can reposition through the evening has far better odds than someone tied to a single spot.

Self-drive or a guided tour? An honest comparison

There are two good ways to chase the aurora, and each suits a different kind of traveller. If you have a rental car and feel confident driving in winter, your own vehicle gives you complete freedom. You set the schedule, you can chase a clear sky using the cloud map, and you can linger as long as you like. For many people this is the most enjoyable and least expensive way to do it.

A guided northern-lights tour, on the other hand, has its own strengths. The guides know the region, watch the forecast all day, and drive the group to wherever the odds of clear sky are best that evening. Many operators also offer a free rebooking if nothing shows up, so you get another attempt. This is an especially good option for those who would rather not drive on Icelandic roads in the dark of winter, or who want to learn from locals.

That said, nobody can promise the aurora — not a guide and not a car. The difference comes down to comfort, safety and how much of the responsibility you want to carry yourself. Neither option changes the weather.

Photographing the aurora — the exact settings that work

The camera often captures far more than the eye can see, so photography is well worth the effort. The essential gear is a tripod, because the exposure is too long to hold the camera steady by hand, and a camera that lets you shoot in manual mode.

Use a wide, fast lens, ideally with an aperture of f/2.8 or lower (a lower number lets in more light). Set your ISO roughly in the 800 to 3200 range depending on how faint the lights are. The shutter speed is usually somewhere between 5 and 20 seconds: shorter for bright, fast-moving aurora so it does not blur into a green haze, longer for faint, slow-moving displays. Focus manually to infinity — autofocus rarely copes with the dark; aim at a bright star and fine-tune until it is sharp.

Shoot in RAW so you can process the images afterwards, and use a timer or remote release to avoid the shake of pressing the button. Carry spare batteries and keep them close to your body, because cold drains batteries quickly. Finally, modern phones with Night Mode capture the aurora remarkably well. Brace the phone against something steady, turn on Night Mode, and hold it still for a few seconds.

What to wear and staying comfortable while you wait

Aurora hunting is largely about waiting outside in the cold, often for hours, and the cold quickly drains the joy out of it. The key is layers. Start with warm base layers of wool or a synthetic that holds heat, add a warm mid-layer or fleece, and finish with a windproof and waterproof outer shell, because the Icelandic wind is relentless.

Do not neglect your extremities. A hat, gloves and warm, waterproof shoes or boots make all the difference, because you lose heat fastest through your head, hands and feet. Wool socks are worth it. A thermos of hot cocoa or tea makes the wait cosy and gives you a reason to stay out just a little longer.

One of the loveliest ways to watch the aurora is from a warm pool or lagoon under the open sky. Lying in hot water with a starry sky overhead combines two things Iceland does very well, and you never feel the cold while you wait.

Realistic expectations — what your eyes actually see

It is important to start with the right expectations. The aurora is a natural phenomenon and comes with no guarantee, no matter how well you prepare. You can do everything right — clear sky, dark spot, good activity — and still see little or nothing on a given night. That is part of the experience.

Something else that surprises many people is how different the lights look to the naked eye versus in a photo. The eye struggles to read colour in the dark, so faint aurora often appears as a greyish-green or even grey-white haze that could be mistaken for thin cloud. In a photograph, which gathers light over several seconds, those same lights burst out in vivid green and purple. This does not mean the camera is lying — it is simply more sensitive than the eye. When activity is strong, you do see bright colour and motion with the naked eye too.

Give yourself more than one night if you can. Someone in Iceland for a single night is entirely at the mercy of the weather; someone with three or four nights has far better odds. Above all, do not fall for promises of “guaranteed” northern lights — no one can guarantee nature, and anyone who insists too firmly is not being honest with you.

Icelandic folklore and culture around the aurora

The northern lights have accompanied Icelanders since the settlement and left their mark on folk belief, even though people of earlier times did not understand the science behind them. These stories are a charming cultural layer to the experience, and they are best taken as folklore rather than fact.

One of the best-known beliefs held that pregnant women should not look at the northern lights, lest the child be born cross-eyed or deformed. Set against that was another belief that the lights could ease childbirth and lessen a woman's pain. These contradictory ideas lived side by side, as old folklore often does.

Another widespread superstition warned against whistling at or waving to the aurora. It was said that if you drew attention to yourself the lights might come too close, snatch you up, or even take your head off. Such tales exist in many forms across the far north, among the Sámi and other peoples, where the lights were variously thought to be the souls of the dead or messengers from another world. Today these are, first and foremost, good stories to tell while you wait beneath a dancing sky.

A quick planning checklist

Here is a summary to keep handy. First, choose travel dates within the season from late August to mid-April, and ideally give yourself three to four nights to improve your odds.

Each day, check the Met Office aurora forecast at vedur.is: find the white (clear) patches on the cloud map and note the activity number, but remember that a clear sky matters more than a high number. Decide whether you will chase the lights in your own car or book a guided tour.

In the evening, get away from town lights to a dark, open spot such as Þingvellir, the South Coast or the open countryside. Dress in layers with a windproof and waterproof outer shell, a hat and gloves, and bring a thermos. Give your eyes fifteen to twenty minutes to adjust to the dark. Have a camera on a tripod set to manual, or a phone with Night Mode, ready to go. And finally, be patient, keep your expectations realistic, and enjoy the night whether or not the lights appear.

Frequently asked questions

Can you see the northern lights in summer?

No. Although activity happens year-round, Iceland's midnight sun keeps the sky too bright through high summer for it ever to get dark enough. The season runs roughly from late August to mid-April, and in June and July you will not see them no matter how strong the activity is.

What is a good Kp number for Iceland?

In Iceland you do not need a high number. Because the country sits directly beneath the auroral oval, activity of just 2 or 3 can produce a stunning display if the sky is clear. A clear sky genuinely matters more than a high activity number.

Can I see them from Reykjavík?

Yes, when activity is strong enough they can be seen even over the city, though light pollution dims the fainter displays. Spots such as Grótta and Öskjuhlíð are better than the city centre, but a short drive out of town gives a much clearer view.

How many nights should I plan?

Ideally three to four. A single night leaves you entirely at the mercy of the weather, whereas several nights greatly increase your chances of catching one that is both clear and active.

Why do they look grey to my eyes but green in photos?

The eye struggles to read colour in the dark, so faint aurora often looks like a greyish or grey-white haze. A camera gathers light over several seconds and captures the colour your eye misses. When activity is strong, you do see vivid colour with the naked eye too.

Do I need a guided tour?

No. If you have a car and feel confident driving in winter, you can chase a clear sky on your own. A guided tour is a good option for those who would rather not drive, since the guides know the region and many offer a free rebooking if nothing shows up.

What is the best month?

Many people point to September to October and February to March, when there is already plenty of darkness but the weather and cloud cover are often more forgiving and the temperatures more bearable than in midwinter. In truth, any dark and clear night in the season can be excellent.

Can my phone photograph them?

Yes. Modern phones with Night Mode capture the aurora remarkably well. Brace the phone against something steady or use a small tripod, turn on Night Mode, and hold it still for a few seconds.

Are they ever visible during the day?

The aurora is there, but daylight overwhelms it completely, so you cannot see it until the sky is dark enough. That is why aurora hunting is always about dark nights during the winter season.
