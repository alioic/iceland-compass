Snæfellsnes – Iceland in Miniature

If you have just one day to spare outside Reykjavík and want to see as much of what makes Iceland special as possible, Snæfellsnes is probably the best answer. The peninsula is often called "Iceland in miniature," and that nickname is well earned. In a relatively small area you will find a glacier-capped volcano, rugged lava fields, black sand and pebble beaches, tall bird cliffs, waterfalls, tiny fishing villages, and sweeping views across Breiðafjörður bay with its countless islands. Few other regions in the country pack so much into such a short loop.

Towering over it all is Snæfellsjökull, a glacier-capped volcano roughly 1,446 metres high that can be seen from far away – even from Reykjavík on a clear day. The mountain is not just beautiful; it is world-famous in literature. The French author Jules Verne chose Snæfellsjökull as the entrance to the centre of the Earth in his 1864 novel Journey to the Center of the Earth. Many Icelanders also associate the mountain with mystery and energy, and it has long held a special place in the national imagination.

What makes Snæfellsnes so convenient for travellers is how accessible everything is. Route 54, the Snæfellsnesvegur, runs a full loop around the peninsula, and most of the main sights sit right beside the road or a short distance from it. You can drive a complete circle and experience remarkably varied landscapes without ever straying far from the route.

Getting There and Driving Around the Peninsula

By far the best way to explore Snæfellsnes is with your own rental car. Public transport is limited and the most beautiful spots are spread out around the peninsula, so the freedom to stop where you want, for as long as you want, makes all the difference. Organised day tours from Reykjavík do exist, but they inevitably cover only a fraction of what the peninsula has to offer.

From Reykjavík the route heads north on Route 1, through the Hvalfjörður tunnel, up to the town of Borgarnes. That leg takes about an hour. In Borgarnes you turn onto Route 54 and head out onto the peninsula itself. Driving from Reykjavík all the way to the western tip, near Arnarstapi and the national park, takes roughly two and a half hours if you drive straight through without stopping – though of course nobody drives straight through, since stopping is the whole point.

The great advantage of Snæfellsnes is that nearly all the key sites lie beside a paved road. Route 54 is surfaced the entire way around. A few short spur roads to individual spots, such as down to some beaches or out to particular farms, may be gravel, but they are usually passable for a regular car in summer. In winter conditions can change quickly, so it is always wise to check road.is before setting off. For a normal summer loop an ordinary car is more than enough; four-wheel drive is rarely necessary unless the weather is poor.

How Long to Spend

It is possible to see Snæfellsnes as a very long day trip from Reykjavík, and many people do exactly that. If you set off early, around seven or eight in the morning, you can drive the loop, stop at the main sights, and be home in the evening. It will be a long day with a lot of driving and little rest, however, and you will be forced to pick and choose and skip some things.

A much better approach, if you can manage it, is to give the peninsula two days and spend a night in the area. That lets you take things at a gentler pace, walk the coastal path between Arnarstapi and Hellnar without rushing, wait for better light for photos, and enjoy the evening atmosphere in the little villages. Good places to stay include Grundarfjörður, Stykkishólmur, Ólafsvík, and around Hellnar and Arnarstapi.

With two days, a common plan is to drive out to the western tip on the first day, stay overnight in somewhere like Grundarfjörður or Ólafsvík, and then explore Kirkjufell, Stykkishólmur, and the north side on the second day on the way back. If you have even more time, three days or more, you open up the possibility of longer walks, the ferry out to Flatey island, and a more relaxed look at lesser-known corners.

Ytri-Tunga – Seals by the Shore

One of the first major stops as you drive the south coast of the peninsula westward is Ytri-Tunga, a golden beach that is one of the best places in the country to see seals in their natural surroundings. Seals often haul out onto skerries and rocks just off the shore and lounge in the open, especially during the summer months.

Nothing can be guaranteed with wild animals, but the chances of seeing seals here are unusually good, and many visitors are lucky. It is best to bring binoculars, keep a respectful distance, and avoid disturbing the animals. Getting too close both stresses the seals and spoils the experience for everyone else. Plan for around half an hour to an hour here, depending on what is happening.

Bear in mind that the beach changes with the tides and seals are more active at certain times. There is no guarantee of when the animals will be present, but a calm morning or afternoon often works well.

Búðir – The Black Church and Búðahraun

A little further west stands one of the peninsula's most beloved photo subjects: Búðakirkja, a small black timber church that stands alone in the Búðahraun lava field with the glacier and the sea behind it. The black church is among the most photographed buildings in Iceland, and for good reason – the contrast between the coal-black church, the green moss, and the vast open landscape is unforgettable.

The church standing here today has a long history; a church has stood on this spot on and off for centuries, and the current building is a reconstruction of an earlier one. This is a working church and a peaceful place, so walk around with respect, especially if a service is under way.

Surrounding the church is Búðahraun, a rugged, moss-covered lava field that is a pleasure to explore. Short walking paths lead through the lava and down to the shore. It is an ideal place to stretch your legs, take photos, and find some quiet. Plan for a brief stop, though many people end up lingering longer than they intended.

Arnarstapi and Hellnar – The Coastal Walk

The villages of Arnarstapi and Hellnar sit side by side on the south coast of the peninsula, beneath Snæfellsjökull, and between them runs one of the loveliest short coastal walks in the country. The path is about two and a half kilometres one way and follows the edge of sea cliffs where the ocean has sculpted basalt columns, rock arches, and deep ravines. The area is a protected natural monument and seabirds nest in the cliffs, so in summer the whole place teems with life and sound.

At Arnarstapi is Gatklettur, a magnificent arched rock that rises out of the sea and is one of the best-known formations in the area. Nearby stands a large stone statue of Bárður Snæfellsás, a half-troll from the medieval Saga of Bárður who was said to be the guardian spirit of the peninsula. The statue is the work of the artist Ragnar Kjartansson and is a popular stopping point.

The walk can be done in either direction and suits most people; it is relatively easy but runs close to steep sea cliffs, so stay well back from the edges, especially in wind. Allow around an hour there and back if you walk at a relaxed pace and stop for photos. Both villages have facilities and good places to grab a coffee or a bite.

Lóndrangar and Djúpalónssandur

Further west on the peninsula, within the national park, Lóndrangar rise from the lava by the shore: two striking basalt pinnacles, the remains of old volcanic vents that the sea and weather have carved into towers. The taller pinnacle is roughly 75 metres high. A short walk leads to a viewpoint with a clear view over the pinnacles and the birdlife around them, making this a quick but memorable stop.

Not far away lies Djúpalónssandur, a dramatic black pebble beach ringed by lava formations and cliffs. A short but scenic path leads down to the shore through unusual terrain. On the beach lie four old lifting stones that fishermen once used to test their strength before earning a place on the rowing boats from the fishing station at Dritvík. The stones are named Amlóði, Hálfdrættingur, Hálfsterkur, and Fullsterkur, and their weights range from about 23 kilograms up to around 154 kilograms. Lifting Hálfdrættingur was considered the minimum to be counted as fit for a crew.

Scattered across the black beach are rusted iron remains from a British trawler that was wrecked here in 1948 with heavy loss of life. The remains have been left in place as a memorial to the disaster, so they should be left undisturbed and not moved. Take care near the water, too: the ocean swell here is extremely dangerous and surges far up the beach, so keep well back from the waterline.

Snæfellsjökull National Park – Vatnshellir, Saxhóll and the Glacier

The whole western tip of the peninsula lies within Snæfellsjökull National Park, established in 2001 and covering roughly 170 square kilometres. It is the only national park in Iceland that stretches continuously from the seashore up to a glacier summit, and within it you will find beaches, lava fields, craters, bird cliffs, and the glacier itself. The park is the heart of the peninsula, and many of the best sites lie within its boundaries.

One of the most enjoyable stops is Vatnshellir, an old lava cave formed by an eruption thousands of years ago. You can only descend into the cave on a guided tour, since it involves climbing down a spiral staircase, and the underground landscape is mysterious and beautiful. Tours usually take under an hour and suit most people able to manage stairs; dress warmly, as it is cool down there year round.

Nearby stands Saxhóll, a neat and beautifully shaped crater that is easy to climb thanks to a metal staircase running up its slope. The climb takes only a few minutes, but the view over the lava fields, the coast, and the glacier is superb. Snæfellsjökull itself, about 1,446 metres high, presides over everything. Climbing the glacier is a task for experienced people with the right equipment or on an organised guided tour, but most visitors are content to admire it from below, which is a worthy reason to visit in its own right.

Kirkjufell and Kirkjufellsfoss

On the north side of the peninsula, just west of the town of Grundarfjörður, rises Kirkjufell, a cone-shaped mountain that many consider the most photographed mountain in Iceland. Its name, meaning "church mountain," refers to its shape, which resembles a church steeple. Right by the car park is a small waterfall, Kirkjufellsfoss, and the classic image is composed with the waterfall in the foreground and Kirkjufell rising behind it. It is without doubt one of Iceland's most recognisable visual icons.

Many people associate Kirkjufell with the television series Game of Thrones, and rightly so – the mountain appeared as the so-called "arrowhead mountain" north of the Wall in seasons six and seven. It is worth keeping in mind, though, that the mountain was only one of many Icelandic filming locations for the series, so it is an exaggeration to call it "the Game of Thrones mountain" as you sometimes hear. It stood magnificently on its own merits long before any television camera came near it.

It takes only a short while to admire Kirkjufell and the waterfall from the car park. Those wanting more can walk the paths around them, but climbing the mountain itself is steep, dangerous in places, and not for the inexperienced – accidents have happened there. For most people the beauty is best enjoyed from the ground, and in winter this is one of the very best places in the country to see and photograph the northern lights.

Stykkishólmur and Surroundings – Bjarnarhöfn and Berserkjahraun

On the northeastern corner of the peninsula stands Stykkishólmur, the liveliest town on Snæfellsnes and a perfect place to stay, eat, and take a stroll. The town is known for its colourful old timber houses and its cosy harbour. On the island of Súgandisey, connected to the mainland by a causeway, stands a small orange lighthouse with wonderful views over Breiðafjörður and its scattered islands. The Norwegian House, one of the oldest two-storey timber buildings in the country, houses a regional museum and is well worth a visit.

From Stykkishólmur the ferry Baldur crosses Breiðafjörður, stopping at the island of Flatey, a unique gem where time seems to have stood still. A day trip or overnight stay on Flatey is unforgettable for those with the time, with rich birdlife and a tranquillity found nowhere else.

Between Stykkishólmur and Grundarfjörður are several more places of interest. At Bjarnarhöfn there is a small museum devoted to shark curing, where you can learn how the traditional fermented shark, hákarl, is made and taste it – an experience that is both cultural and memorable. Nearby lies Berserkjahraun, a historic and beautiful moss-covered lava field linked to warriors from the old sagas. On the western side of the peninsula are the fishing villages of Ólafsvík, Rif, and Hellissandur, genuine working sea towns where boats still put out and you can enjoy a meal of fresh fish.

The Best Time of Year to Visit Snæfellsnes

Snæfellsnes is a year-round destination, but the experience is completely different depending on the season. Summer, from June into August, is the easiest time: all roads are open, the weather is milder, the days are long and bright, and around midsummer it barely gets dark at all. This is when the bird cliffs are alive, and in some spots you may see puffins, though Snæfellsnes is not the country's most famous puffin site. Summer is also the busy season, so the main sights can be crowded in the middle of the day.

Autumn and spring are quieter and often very beautiful, with ever-changing light and colours, but the weather can be unpredictable and the first or last snows can affect driving conditions. In these shoulder seasons you can have many lovely spots almost to yourself.

Winter is dramatic and captivating. The snow-covered glacier and Kirkjufell beneath the northern lights are among the most sought-after photo subjects in the country, and Snæfellsnes is one of the best places to chase the aurora thanks to its low light pollution. But winter demands respect: the days are short, daylight is scarce, the weather can change in an instant, and some roads and spur routes become harder or close temporarily. In winter you should always follow the weather and road forecasts closely and give yourself plenty of time.

Where to Stay and Eat

Although Snæfellsnes is popular, it is still relatively sparsely populated, so it pays to book accommodation in good time, especially over the summer and if you want to stay in a particular place. The main towns with accommodation and services are Stykkishólmur and Grundarfjörður on the north side and Ólafsvík on the west side, along with smaller lodgings around Hellnar and Arnarstapi on the south side. Your choice depends on how you want to plan the loop.

Stykkishólmur and Grundarfjörður are convenient bases if you want to be near Kirkjufell, the ferry, and the northern part, while Hellnar, Arnarstapi, and Ólafsvík suit those who want to be closer to the national park and the western tip. All of these towns offer accommodation of various kinds, from guesthouses and farm stays up to hotels.

As for food, seafood takes centre stage, as these are fishing towns. In the harbour towns you will find fresh fish and often excellent fish soup, and small restaurants and cafés are dotted around the peninsula. The choice is more limited than in Reykjavík, however, and many places close early or keep shorter hours outside the peak season, so it is wise not to count on finding somewhere open late in the evening. It is a good idea to keep some snacks and a drink in the car, particularly if you are travelling outside the high season.

Safety and Practical Tips

Snæfellsnes is safe and accessible, but Icelandic nature has a habit of catching people off guard, so a few basics matter. The weather and wind can change very quickly, even in summer, and gusts have damaged open car doors and knocked people off their feet. Check the weather forecast on vedur.is, road conditions on road.is, and safety information on safetravel.is before you set off, and adapt your plan to the conditions rather than the other way around.

By the sea and on cliffs, one simple rule applies: stay away from the edges and away from the waterline. The sea cliffs at Arnarstapi and Hellnar are steep, the ground can be loose, and the swell at Djúpalónssandur is extremely dangerous. Never step near an edge to get a better photo; it is simply not worth it. Likewise, the steep and loose climb up mountains such as Kirkjufell is not for the inexperienced.

Driving in Iceland calls for attention. Stay on the road, as off-road driving is both illegal and damaging to fragile nature. Slow down on gravel stretches and single-lane bridges. Fuel and charging stations are scarce on the peninsula, so fill up or charge whenever you get the chance, for example in Borgarnes, Stykkishólmur, Grundarfjörður, or Ólafsvík, and do not assume the next station will be open. With these simple points in mind, nothing stands in the way of an unforgettable trip around the country's most beautiful peninsula.

Frequently asked questions

Can you see Snæfellsnes as a day trip from Reykjavík?

Yes, it is possible but it makes for a long day. The driving alone is about five hours round trip, plus stops, so you need to set off early and be selective about the sights. To truly enjoy the peninsula, a two-day trip with one overnight stay is a far better option.

Do I need a four-wheel-drive car for Snæfellsnes?

No, not in summer. Route 54 is paved the whole way around and most of the main sites sit on surfaced roads, so an ordinary car is fine. In winter conditions can deteriorate quickly, and then four-wheel drive and good tyres are an advantage; always check road.is for winter driving.

Did Kirkjufell appear in Game of Thrones?

Yes. Kirkjufell featured as the so-called "arrowhead mountain" north of the Wall in seasons six and seven. It was only one of many Icelandic filming locations, however, so it is an overstatement to single it out as "the Game of Thrones mountain."

Can I climb Snæfellsjökull?

Climbing all the way onto the glacier is a task for experienced people with the right equipment, or best done on an organised guided tour, as crevasses and weather can be dangerous. Most visitors are content to admire the glacier from below and take shorter walks such as up Saxhóll, which offers a superb view.

When is the best time to see seals at Ytri-Tunga?

Seals are most often seen at Ytri-Tunga during the summer months when they haul out onto the skerries by the shore. Nothing can be guaranteed with wild animals, though. Bring binoculars, keep your distance, and expect slightly better odds around low tide.

Can you see the northern lights on Snæfellsnes?

Yes, Snæfellsnes is one of the best aurora spots in the country thanks to its low light pollution, and Kirkjufell beneath the northern lights is a classic image. The aurora is only visible during the darker months, roughly from September into April, and only when the sky is clear and activity is sufficient.

What is fermented shark and can I taste it on Snæfellsnes?

Fermented shark, or hákarl, is a traditional Icelandic food made from Greenland shark that is cured and dried over many months. At Bjarnarhöfn on the north side of the peninsula there is a small museum where you can learn about the curing process and taste the shark – a strong but memorable experience.

Which town is best to stay in on Snæfellsnes?

It depends on your route. Stykkishólmur and Grundarfjörður are well placed for being near Kirkjufell, the ferry, and the north, while Ólafsvík, Hellnar, and Arnarstapi are closer to the national park and the western tip. Book early in summer, as availability is limited.
