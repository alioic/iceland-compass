What to Pack for Iceland: The Complete Season-by-Season Guide

A thorough Iceland packing guide: the layering system, the right gear and clear checklists for summer, winter and the shoulder seasons.

The golden rule: the weather rules, and it changes in an instant

If you remember only one thing from this guide, make it this: in Iceland you can experience four seasons in a single day. The morning can be calm and sunny, midday windy with driving rain, and the late afternoon bright but bitingly cold again. This is true all year round, including July. Wind is almost always present, and it changes everything: ten degrees Celsius in still air feels pleasant, but the same ten degrees in a sharp northerly with rain bites hard.

Because of this, packing for Iceland is not about predicting the exact temperature. It is about being ready for anything. You pack for wind, rain and sudden cold no matter which month you arrive. The travellers who have a miserable time in Iceland are almost always the ones who trusted the forecast and packed too light, not the ones who brought a little too much.

The key word is layering. Instead of bringing one thick coat and hoping for the best, you dress in several thin layers that you can add or remove as conditions change. When you hike uphill you warm up and shed a layer; when you stop at a windy waterfall you add one back. This system is simple, light and works in every season. The rest of this guide explains exactly how to build it.

The layering system explained: base, insulation and shell

Good layering rests on three layers that work together. The first, the base layer, sits next to your skin and its job is to wick sweat away from your body so you stay dry. Here the fabric matters more than anything: choose wool or merino wool, or a synthetic technical fabric designed to dry fast. A wool base layer keeps insulating even when it gets damp and smells far less after several days of wear.

The second layer is the insulating or mid layer, which keeps you warm. A fleece or a thin wool jumper works well, and a light down vest or thin down jacket is an excellent choice because it packs down small yet gives a lot of warmth. You can carry two such layers and add one when it turns cold. An insulating layer works by trapping air that your body heats, so something loose and lofty insulates better than something tight.

The third layer is the shell, the outermost layer that protects you from wind and water. This is the single most important garment for the trip: a windproof and waterproof jacket with an attached hood. Look for a jacket that is genuinely waterproof with taped seams, not merely water resistant. Waterproof over-trousers that you can pull on over your normal trousers are equally invaluable on rainy days and at waterfalls where the spray drifts over you.

Why not cotton? There is an old but sound rule among outdoor people: cotton kills. Cotton soaks up moisture, whether sweat or rain, holds it against your skin and stops insulating. A wet cotton shirt in the wind chills the body fast and in the worst case can lead to hypothermia. Swap cotton shirts, jeans and cotton socks for wool or technical fabrics and you will feel the difference at once.

Footwear: the foundation people forget

Your feet carry you through the whole trip, and wet, cold feet ruin a day faster than almost anything else. The essential item is a pair of sturdy, waterproof walking or hiking boots that rise above the ankle and give good grip. Ankle support matters, because Icelandic trails are often rocky, wet and uneven, and lava fields can be especially treacherous underfoot.

Important: break your boots in at home before you travel. Brand new hiking boots worn for the first time in Iceland often end the first day in blisters and sore feet. Wear them on a few walks at home so they mould to your feet.

Wool socks matter as much as the boots themselves. Bring more pairs than you think you need, because a dry pair to change into is worth its weight in gold once the first pair gets wet. Wool socks keep you warm even when damp and are far better than cotton.

Bring a spare pair of shoes as well. Light trainers or comfortable shoes for wandering around town, for restaurants, and for when the big boots are wet. And do not forget flip-flops or pool sandals: they are essential for swimming pools and hot pots, which you will visit more often than you expect.

Summer packing (June to August)

The Icelandic summer is wonderful, but it is not a beach holiday. Temperatures usually sit between ten and fifteen degrees Celsius and rarely climb above twenty. Bright days can be warm and pleasant, but the moment wind or rain arrives it cools quickly. That is why you still bring warm layers in summer, tempting as it is to pack light.

The core kit for summer is the same as all year: a wool base layer, a fleece or light wool jumper, and above all a windproof and waterproof shell. Add a hat and light gloves, because in the evening, up in the highlands or near glaciers it can turn cold even in July. Waterproof trousers belong in the bag all summer long.

Summer's special feature is the midnight sun. In June and into July it barely gets dark and the sun hardly sets. This is magical but can wreck your sleep. Bring a good eye mask to block out the light; it is one of the best small investments for the trip. Sunglasses are useful too, since a low sun can shine straight into your eyes late into the evening.

Do not forget swimwear. Summer is a perfect time for swimming pools, natural pools and hot pots, and you will always want your swimsuit within reach. A light t-shirt and shorts have their place on warm days, but treat them as an addition to the warm layers, not a replacement for them.

Winter packing (November to March)

Winter in Iceland is less about biting frost and more about wind, wet and ice. Temperatures in inhabited areas are often near freezing and rarely very low near the coast, but the wind makes it feel much colder than the numbers suggest. Damp works its way in and the darkness is long, so good gear makes all the difference.

The outermost layer in winter is an insulated, waterproof coat that comes down well and keeps the wind out. Underneath it you wear warm wool base layers and one or two insulating layers. A warm hat that covers your ears, good gloves (ideally waterproof) and a scarf or buff to protect your neck and face are essential. A buff is especially useful because you can pull it up over your nose and cheeks in the worst gusts.

Your boots need to be waterproof and insulated, because cold, wet feet in freezing weather quickly ruin a day. On top of that I strongly recommend ice grips, small spikes (often called microspikes or crampons) that snap onto the soles of your boots. Pavements, car parks and paths regularly turn to sheet ice in winter, and ice grips are the difference between a safe walk and a fall. They are light, cheap and take up no space.

Hand warmers, small single-use packets that heat up when you open them, are a pleasant luxury on cold days, especially if you plan to stand outside waiting for the northern lights. Bring warm pyjamas too, because not every guesthouse is toasty, and a good sleep in the warm makes all the difference.

Spring and autumn (April to May, September to October)

The shoulder seasons are the most unpredictable of all. In spring you can get a sunny, mild day and snow the next, and autumn swings from mild golden days to the first blasts of winter. The rule here is simple: pack for both summer and winter and be ready for anything.

In practice this means bringing the full layering system: wool base layers, a fleece and an insulating layer, and a sturdy windproof and waterproof shell. Add a hat and gloves as you would in winter, but keep lighter layers too for the mild days. Waterproof boots and rain trousers are still essential.

Autumn, especially from September, is a fine time for the northern lights, because the darkness is lengthening while the weather is often milder than midwinter. If you plan to stand outside in the evening waiting for the lights, bring an extra layer, a hat and gloves, because standing still in the dark chills you fast. In spring the daylight lengthens quickly in return, so an eye mask can earn its place by May.

Bear in mind that conditions vary a lot depending on where you are in the country. The south and west coasts are milder, while the highlands and the north can stay wintry well into spring. If you plan to go high above sea level or up north, pack as if for winter.

Swimwear and pool kit

If there is one garment no one should forget for Iceland, it is swimwear. Swimming pools are at the heart of Icelandic community life and are found in almost every single town, heated all year round. On top of that there are natural pools, geothermal baths and heitir pottar (hot pots, the outdoor hot tubs found all over Iceland) across the country, and some of the most memorable moments of the trip happen in warm water under the open sky, even in falling snow.

Keep your swimsuit somewhere accessible, not buried at the bottom of a bag, because the opportunities often come unexpectedly. It is also handy to own two swimsuits in case one has not dried between visits.

Towels are expensive to rent at many pools, so it pays to bring your own. A light microfibre towel that dries fast and packs down small is ideal for travelling. Flip-flops or pool sandals are comfortable on the wet tiles around the pools.

One thing worth knowing: in Iceland there is a strict rule that everyone must wash thoroughly, without swimwear, in the shower before swimming. This is part of the culture and the reason the pools need very little chlorine. Do not take it as a personal challenge; it is simply how things are done, and locals appreciate visitors who respect the rule.

The day pack: what you carry every day

A small, comfortable day pack is invaluable for daily outings, whether you are hiking, moving between sites or heading to a waterfall. Into it goes everything you want within reach during the day.

Top of the list is a refillable water bottle. Icelandic tap water is among the cleanest and best in the world, cold, fresh and completely free. You never need to buy bottled water in Iceland; simply fill your bottle from the tap. If you smell sulphur, that is the hot water; let the cold tap run a moment, as the cold tap water is odourless and excellent.

Other useful things for the day pack: a small first-aid kit with plasters (especially for blisters), painkillers and anything you take regularly; a power bank for your phone, since cold drains batteries fast and your phone is your camera and map; sunglasses, because low sun and glare off snow or water can be strong; lip balm and moisturiser, because the wind dries out skin and lips; and a small towel. Snacks and energy bars are wise too, because distances between shops can be long.

Electricity, charging and practical tech

Iceland uses European plugs of type C and F, the same two-pin sockets as most of mainland Europe, and the voltage is 230 volts at 50 hertz. If you come from Europe your devices plug straight in. If you come from the United Kingdom, the United States or elsewhere you will need an adapter. Note that most devices such as phones and cameras handle both 110 and 230 volts, but items like hair dryers do not always, so read the label before you plug in.

Bring a couple of adapters if you carry several devices, because it is annoying to have to choose what gets to charge overnight. A power strip from home can turn one socket into many.

Your camera, whether a phone or a dedicated unit, belongs on this trip because the landscape is spectacular. Protect your electronics from the wet with a simple solution: a waterproof bag or dry bag keeps a phone and camera dry at waterfalls and in the rain. A rechargeable power bank is a must, as mentioned above.

If you plan to drive yourself, a phone mount for the car is very useful for following navigation without holding the phone. Also download offline maps before you set off, because mobile coverage can drop out in remote areas, even though it is generally good across Iceland.

What NOT to over-pack, and what to buy locally

It is easy to bring too much out of fear of the Icelandic weather, but some things are better left behind or bought on the spot. The most famous example is the lopapeysa, the classic Icelandic wool sweater. Do not try to find an imitation at home; a genuine lopapeysa is warm, wind resistant and sold all over the country. Many travellers buy one at the start of the trip and use it as an insulating layer throughout, and it becomes a beautiful souvenir along the way.

You also do not need to bring a whole medicine cabinet or full-size versions of every toiletry. If you forget toothpaste, sunscreen, plasters or shampoo, all of it is available in shops and pharmacies, especially in the capital area and larger towns. Bring a little of what you use daily and buy the rest if you need it.

What is worth bringing from home, however, is anything expensive or hard to find locally: specialist hiking gear, quality waterproofs, ice grips and any medication you rely on. Iceland is an expensive country and prices for clothing and outdoor equipment are higher than in most places, so it pays to arrive well equipped rather than planning to buy everything on arrival.

One practical tip: if you bring outdoor clothing you would rather not carry home wet and dirty, pack a few plastic bags or dry bags to keep the wet apart from the dry in your luggage.

Season-by-season checklist in a nutshell

All year, whatever the season: wool base layers (top and bottom), a fleece or wool jumper, a windproof and waterproof shell (jacket and trousers), waterproof walking boots, several pairs of wool socks, a hat, gloves, swimwear, a microfibre towel, a refillable water bottle, a power bank, an adapter (type C and F), sunglasses and lip balm.

Summer (June to August): add an eye mask for the midnight sun, a light t-shirt and shorts for warm days, but keep the warm layers with you. Sunglasses are handy for the low evening sun.

Winter (November to March): add an insulated, waterproof coat, thicker base layers, a scarf or buff, waterproof and insulated boots, ice grips and hand warmers. Warm pyjamas are a welcome extra.

Spring and autumn (April to May, September to October): pack for both summer and winter. The full layering system, a hat and gloves, an extra layer for northern lights watching in autumn and an eye mask for bright spring nights. Expect anything and you will never be caught out.

Frequently asked questions

Do I really need warm clothes in an Icelandic summer?

Not full winter clothing, but yes, you need warm layers. The Icelandic summer is cool, usually ten to fifteen degrees Celsius, and wind and rain can chill you quickly. Bring a wool base layer, a fleece or jumper, a waterproof shell, a hat and light gloves. You will not use them every day, but you will be glad to have them when the weather turns.

Is Icelandic tap water safe to drink?

Yes, absolutely. Icelandic tap water is among the cleanest in the world, cold and fresh, and it is free. Bring a refillable bottle and fill up from the tap; you never need to buy bottled water. If the hot water smells of sulphur that is normal; the cold water is odourless.

What plugs and voltage does Iceland use?

Iceland uses European plugs of type C and F with 230 volts at 50 hertz. Travellers from mainland Europe need no adapter. From the United Kingdom, the United States and elsewhere you will need an adapter, and check that your devices handle 230 volts before you plug them in.

Do I need ice grips in Iceland?

In winter, roughly November to March, I strongly recommend them. Pavements and paths often turn to sheet ice, and ice grips that snap onto your boots make walking safe. They are light, cheap and take up almost no space. In summer you generally do not need them except on glaciers or high mountains.

Why should I avoid cotton?

Cotton soaks up moisture, whether sweat or rain, holds it against your skin and stops insulating. Wet cotton in the wind chills the body fast and can lead to hypothermia. That is why the old rule says cotton kills. Choose wool, merino or fast-drying technical fabrics instead for your base layers, shirts and socks.

Should I buy a lopapeysa before I go or in Iceland?

In Iceland. A genuine lopapeysa, the classic Icelandic wool sweater, is warm, wind resistant and sold all over the country, and it makes a lovely souvenir. Many travellers buy one at the start of the trip and use it as an insulating layer throughout. Imitations from home give neither the same warmth nor the same experience.

What do I need to bring for the pools and hot pots?

Swimwear, a fast-drying microfibre towel and flip-flops or pool sandals. Swimming pools are found in almost every town and natural pools are all over the country, so keep your swimsuit accessible. Note that in Iceland you must wash thoroughly without swimwear in the shower before swimming; it is part of the culture.

Can I buy things I forget once I am in Iceland?

Yes, everyday items like toothpaste, sunscreen, plasters and shampoo are available in shops and pharmacies, especially in the capital area and larger towns. But Iceland is expensive, and specialist outdoor gear, quality waterproofs and ice grips cost more than in most places, so it pays to arrive well equipped from home with what matters.
