Ásbyrgi

A horseshoe-shaped canyon with cliffs up to 100 metres, a still pond and birch woodland.

Ásbyrgi is a spectacular horseshoe-shaped canyon in the northern part of Jökulsárgljúfur, within Vatnajökull National Park. Folklore says it formed when Sleipnir, Odin's eight-legged horse, set down a hoof, though geologists trace it to catastrophic glacial floods in the river Jökulsá á Fjöllum. At its sheltered innermost end, cliffs rise up to 100 metres above the pond Botnstjörn, ringed by lush birch forest and walking trails.

Ásbyrgi: the horseshoe canyon in Vatnajökull National Park

Ásbyrgi is one of Northeast Iceland's most striking natural wonders, a horseshoe-shaped canyon in the northern part of Jökulsárgljúfur within Vatnajökull National Park. The canyon is roughly 3.5 kilometres long and just over a kilometre wide, framed by sheer cliffs that rise to around 100 metres at its innermost end. A rock outcrop called Eyjan, or the Island, stands in the middle and splits the canyon into two arms. Geologists attribute its formation to catastrophic glacial floods in the river Jökulsá á Fjöllum thousands of years ago.

The Sleipnir legend behind Ásbyrgi

Icelandic folklore holds that Ásbyrgi was formed when Sleipnir, Odin's eight-legged horse, set down one hoof and left a horseshoe-shaped imprint on the land. The story lends the canyon a sense of mystery and fits its shape when seen from the cliff tops above. Geology instead traces the canyon to enormous glacial outburst floods that surged down the course of Jökulsá á Fjöllum. Ásbyrgi is also known in Icelandic tradition as a capital of the hidden people, or elves. Either way, its towering walls and stillness leave a lasting impression.

Walking trails in Ásbyrgi and Botnstjörn pond

At the innermost end of Ásbyrgi, beneath the highest cliffs, lies the sheltered pond Botnstjörn, surrounded by lush birch woodland. An easy trail leads to a viewing deck at the pond, where ducks and other birds gather, and in spring a small waterfall spills down the rock face. A range of trails cross the canyon, from short loops to longer routes up onto Eyjan and south through Jökulsárgljúfur. The birch forest on the canyon floor is among the most verdant in North Iceland, giving the site a uniquely calm atmosphere.

Visiting Ásbyrgi: access, seasons and where to stay

Ásbyrgi lies about an hour's drive from Húsavík and Mývatn and forms part of the popular Diamond Circle route. A paved road runs into the canyon, with car parks at the Gljúfrastofa visitor centre and at Botnstjörn. The canyon itself is accessible year-round, while services, the visitor centre and campsite mainly operate from spring into autumn. The campsite at Ásbyrgi is a favourite base, and a wider choice of hotels and guesthouses can be found around Mývatn and in Húsavík. Allow a few hours to explore the canyon properly.
