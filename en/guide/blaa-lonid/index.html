The Blue Lagoon: A Complete Guide to Iceland's Most Famous Spa

Everything you need to know about the Blue Lagoon on the Reykjanes peninsula: what it is, how to book, etiquette, best times, and alternatives if it closes.

What Is the Blue Lagoon? A Geothermal Spa, Not a Natural Wonder

The Blue Lagoon is Iceland's most famous bathing spot and one of the country's single most popular attractions. It sits in the middle of a black lava field on the Reykjanes peninsula, surrounded by rugged, moss-covered terrain that looks like another planet. The milky-blue color of the water, which gives the lagoon its name, comes from silica and other minerals that scatter light in a distinctive way.

It is important to be honest about one thing up front: the Blue Lagoon is not a natural wonder in the traditional sense. The lagoon came into being as a byproduct of the Svartsengi geothermal power plant, which pumps hot geothermal seawater from deep underground to generate electricity and hot water. The runoff water — a mix of seawater and fresh water heated in the earth — collected in the surrounding lava and formed the lagoon we know today.

In other words, this is a man-made lagoon filled with geothermal seawater rich in silica, algae, and minerals. That does not make the experience any less worthwhile — the water really is mineral-rich, and many people notice softer skin afterward — but it is good to arrive with the right expectations rather than imagining a wild hot spring that formed on its own.

Location: Just 20 Minutes From Keflavík Airport

The Blue Lagoon is exceptionally well placed for most travelers. It sits roughly 20 minutes by car from Keflavík International Airport and about 45 minutes from downtown Reykjavík. That location makes it one of the most accessible destinations in the entire country.

The classic tip — and it still holds up — is to visit the Blue Lagoon on your arrival or departure day. If you land early and cannot check into your hotel until the afternoon, it is perfect to make a small detour, let the tiredness of the flight melt away in the warm water, and then continue on to Reykjavík feeling refreshed. The same works on departure day: a soak before you drive out to the airport is a lovely way to say goodbye to the country.

If you have a rental car, it is easy to find — the route is well signposted off Route 41 (Reykjanesbraut), and there is good parking on site. For those without a car, numerous bus companies offer direct transfers from both the airport and Reykjavík, often with flexible timings that match your booked entry slot at the lagoon.

You Must Book in Advance — the Lagoon Often Sells Out

This is the single most important practical piece of advice in this whole guide: you must book your entry to the Blue Lagoon in advance. You cannot simply show up and walk in off the road. Admission runs on timed entry slots, and the most popular times — especially in the afternoon and during high season — regularly sell out days or even weeks ahead.

So book as soon as you know your travel dates. When you book, you choose a time to arrive; you may usually stay as long as you like after that, but the check-in time is binding and it is best to arrive on schedule.

The Blue Lagoon offers different admission tiers or packages. I will not quote prices here, because they change and vary by season and demand — always check the official website for current rates. But in general terms, the more affordable tier includes entrance, a silica mud mask, and usually a towel or a drink, while the higher tiers add a towel, a bathrobe, additional masks, refreshments, and access to premium areas. Look carefully at what is included in each tier before you choose, because the difference is often in the details.

What It's Like: Warm Water, Masks, and the In-Water Bar

The water in the Blue Lagoon is pleasantly warm, typically around 38–39°C (100–102°F), and the temperature is fairly even across the lagoon, though some sections run warmer than others. It is deep enough to wade through but in most places does not rise above your shoulders, so you feel safe even if you are not a strong swimmer.

The signature feature of the experience is the silica masks. A white silica mud mask comes with your admission; you apply it to your face, let it dry, and rinse it off — it is said to cleanse and soften the skin. Higher-tier packages add an algae mask and further treatments. There is also a bar right in the water where you can order a drink — anything from sparkling wine to juice and soft drinks — and enjoy it while you relax in the warm water.

The site also has a sauna and steam room, a cold waterfall to invigorate you, and plenty of spots to unwind. For those who want more privacy and luxury, there is The Retreat, a dedicated premium wing with a private lagoon, an elegant spa, and geothermal seawater treatments. On site there are also two hotels — the Silica Hotel and the upscale Retreat Hotel — and the Lava Restaurant, built into a lava cliff and serving Icelandic cuisine in a striking setting. You can easily make a full day, or even an overnight stay, out of a visit.

Etiquette: Shower Without a Swimsuit Before You Enter

Here is a rule that surprises many foreign visitors but is an absolute fundamental in Iceland: you are expected to shower and wash thoroughly with soap — without a swimsuit — before entering the lagoon. This is not a suggestion but a firm rule at every pool and bathing spot in the country.

The reason is simple. Icelandic pools use far less chlorine than is common elsewhere, and the cleanliness of guests is what keeps the water clean. Showering naked before bathing is a natural part of Icelandic bathing culture and nobody thinks twice about it. There are changing rooms with private showers for those who feel shy, but the core rule stands: wash before you get in.

One more practical tip: use plenty of conditioner and ideally tie your hair up or wear a cap if it is long. The silica in the water is wonderful for your skin but it dries out your hair badly and can leave it stiff and straw-like for days if you are not careful. Work a generous amount of conditioner through your hair before bathing, leave it in, and try to keep your hair out of the water. This small step saves many people from an unpleasant hair experience after an otherwise wonderful visit.

Best Time to Go: Early Morning or Late Evening

The Blue Lagoon is popular year-round and can get quite crowded, especially in the middle of the day when tour buses and groups arrive. If you want a calmer, more personal experience, it is far better to choose the first entry slot of the day in the early morning or the last slot late in the evening.

Early in the morning the water is still, guests are few, and a special calm often settles over the area. In winter you might even be in the warm water in darkness and see the northern lights dance over the lava if you are lucky. Late in the evening, especially in summer when it is bright around the clock, there is also a unique atmosphere.

The season matters too. In summer (June to August) there are longer opening hours and milder weather, but also the most tourists. In winter there are fewer guests during quiet slots, colder air that makes the contrast with the warm water even more enjoyable, and the chance of northern lights. Whatever you choose, avoid the middle of the day if you can and book your slot early, because the best times go first.

Accessibility for Everyone

The Blue Lagoon has invested considerably in being accessible to guests with reduced mobility. There is lift equipment and special facilities to get in and out of the lagoon, accessible changing rooms and toilets, and staff who are used to assisting. If you or someone in your group needs special assistance, it is best to contact the lagoon in advance so they can prepare for your visit as well as possible.

As for children, the lagoon is open to families, but there are age limits and rules worth knowing about. The youngest children are generally not admitted, and children under a certain age must use special equipment such as arm floats and must be accompanied by an adult at all times. Check the official website for the exact age rules before you book for the family, as these can change.

The water itself is mild and safe for most people, but anyone with certain health conditions — for example serious heart or circulatory disease — should consult a doctor before spending a long time in very hot water.

Honesty About Volcanic Activity: Check the Status Before You Book

This is the section no honest guide can skip in 2026. The Reykjanes peninsula, where the Blue Lagoon stands, has been in an active eruption period since 2021, with repeated eruptions near Grindavík and Svartsengi from late 2023 through 2024 and beyond. The Svartsengi area, which powers the lagoon, has repeatedly bulged upward from magma accumulating beneath the surface.

Because of this, the Blue Lagoon has had to close temporarily several times, whether due to an eruption, a seismic swarm, or air quality. These closures have been precautionary and made on the instructions of Iceland's Civil Protection and the Icelandic Meteorological Office, and the lagoon has always reopened once the area has been judged safe. Protective barriers have been built around Svartsengi and robust evacuation plans are in place. In practice, the lagoon has been open the vast majority of the time, even during volcanic activity.

But the conclusion is clear: the situation can change at short notice. Before you book, and again just before your visit, check the official Blue Lagoon website (bluelagoon.com) and safetravel.is for the latest information on status and safety. As this guide is written, the lagoon is open and operating normally, but experts expect the eruption period to continue. So keep flexibility in your travel plans and have a backup ready.

Alternatives: Other Baths if the Lagoon Is Closed (or Just for Variety)

If the Blue Lagoon is closed while you are in the country — or if you simply want to try something else, often at a lower price and with fewer tourists — Iceland is full of excellent alternatives. Happily, the bathing culture here is rich and varied.

Sky Lagoon in Kópavogur is a striking modern bath just outside Reykjavík with spectacular ocean views and a seven-step bathing ritual. Hvammsvík on Hvalfjörður offers natural pools at the water's edge in beautiful surroundings. Krauma, by the Deildartunguhver spring in Borgarfjörður, uses water from Europe's most powerful hot spring. Forest Lagoon near Akureyri sits inside a forest with views over Eyjafjörður. In the north there are also the Mývatn Nature Baths, often called the Blue Lagoon of the north, and the GeoSea sea baths in Húsavík with views over whale-watching waters.

And do not forget the cheapest and most authentically Icelandic option of all: the public swimming pools. Nearly every town in Iceland has a public pool with hot tubs, and they are the heart of Icelandic community life — warm, inexpensive, and full of locals. Frequently asked questions

Do I need to book in advance?

Yes, absolutely. The Blue Lagoon does not accept walk-ins — all admission is on timed entry slots that frequently sell out days or weeks ahead. Book as soon as you know your travel dates, through the official website.

Is the Blue Lagoon natural?

No. The lagoon is man-made and came into being as a byproduct of the Svartsengi geothermal power plant. The water is geothermal seawater, rich in silica and minerals, that collects in the lava field. It is genuinely mineral-rich, but it is not a natural hot spring.

How far is the lagoon from the airport?

The Blue Lagoon is about 20 minutes by car from Keflavík Airport and roughly 45 minutes from downtown Reykjavík. That is why it is classic to visit on your arrival or departure day.

Do I really have to shower naked?

Yes. Showering thoroughly with soap and without a swimsuit before bathing is a firm rule at every Icelandic pool and bathing spot, because the pools use little chlorine and rely on guest cleanliness. There are private showers for those who prefer more privacy.

Is the lagoon open right now, or is it affected by volcanoes?

As this is written, the Blue Lagoon is open and operating normally, but the Reykjanes peninsula is in an active eruption period and the lagoon has closed temporarily several times for safety. Always check bluelagoon.com and safetravel.is for the latest status before you go.

What's included in admission?

It depends on the package. The more affordable tier usually includes entrance, a silica mud mask, and a towel or a drink, while the higher tiers add a bathrobe, extra masks, refreshments, and access to premium areas. Prices change, so check the official website.

What are the best alternatives?

Sky Lagoon in Kópavogur, Hvammsvík, Krauma, Forest Lagoon near Akureyri, the Mývatn Nature Baths, and GeoSea in Húsavík are all excellent baths. The cheapest and most authentic option is the public swimming pools — see our Iceland pools map.

Can I visit with kids?

Yes, the lagoon is open to families, but there are age limits. The youngest children are not admitted, and children under a certain age need arm floats and adult supervision. Check the exact rules on the official website before booking for the family.
