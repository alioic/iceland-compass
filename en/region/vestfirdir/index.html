The Westfjords – the steepest fjords and untouched wilderness

The Westfjords (Vestfirðir) are the most remote and one of the most pristine parts of Iceland, where deep fjords cut into steep mountains and few people live. This is a land of contrasts: sheer bird cliffs, thundering waterfalls, warm natural pools at the sea's edge, and Hornstrandir – a nature reserve uninhabited for decades where the Arctic fox reigns. Because of the distance from the main routes, many travellers skip the Westfjords, and precisely for that reason those who make the journey find some of the quietest and most spectacular wilderness in the country. For anyone seeking solitude, unspoiled nature and off-the-beaten-path adventure, the Westfjords are the highlight of an Iceland trip.

Top places and highlights in the Westfjords

Dynjandi is the Westfjords' most majestic waterfall, a roughly hundred-metre tiered cascade that fans out like a bridal veil over Arnarfjörður, with six smaller falls below. Látrabjarg, the westernmost point of Iceland and Europe, is a fourteen-kilometre bird cliff where the puffins are so tame you can get right up close, with the golden beach of Rauðisandur nearby. Ísafjörður, the region's capital, charms with the country's oldest houses, a lively music scene and views up to the Naustahvilft cirque. Further north lie Hornstrandir, with the Hornbjarg cliff and untouched abandoned settlements, while on the Strandir coast, Hólmavík is home to the singular Museum of Icelandic Sorcery and Witchcraft.

Getting around the Westfjords

The Westfjords are the most distance- and time-consuming part of the country to visit, as the Ring Road does not pass through them – reaching them requires a dedicated detour to the north. Roads wind around fjords and over mountain passes, and some are still gravel, so driving is slower than elsewhere; allow at least three to five days. From Snæfellsnes, the Baldur ferry shortens the journey across Breiðafjörður. Hornstrandir cannot be reached by car – access is only on foot or by boat from Ísafjörður. In winter many mountain passes close, so the Westfjords are best visited in summer. A capable vehicle and plenty of time are the keys to a Westfjords adventure.

Best time to visit and practical tips

Summer, from June to August, is by far the best time to visit the Westfjords; roads are open, ferries run and birdlife is at its peak – the puffins at Látrabjarg are present from May into August. Hiking in Hornstrandir is mainly possible in July and August when boats from Ísafjörður run regularly. In winter the region is challenging due to snow-heavy mountain passes and limited services. Give yourself ample time, fill up on fuel whenever you can as petrol stations are sparse, and be ready for ever-changing weather. Enjoy the warm natural pools along the way and stay in Ísafjörður or smaller villages to experience Westfjords hospitality.

Places & nature

  • Dynjandi The thundering bridal veil of the Westfjords - a roughly 100 m tiered waterfall.
  • Látrabjarg The westernmost tip of Europe and one of the largest bird cliffs in the world.
  • Hornstrandir A pristine, roadless nature reserve where the Arctic fox reigns supreme.
  • Ísafjörður The largest town in the Westfjords, encircled by steep mountains.
  • Hólmavík A fishing village in the Strandir region, known for the Museum of Sorcery and Witchcraft.

Hiking trails

  • Hornvík og Hornbjarg A multi-day trek through the untouched Hornstrandir to the highest bird cliffs in the country.
  • Naustahvilft A steep climb to the troll seat known as The Pot, with sweeping views over Ísafjörður.
  • Kaldbakur A climb to the Westfjords' highest peak at 998 metres, deep in the Westfjords Alps.
  • Bolafjall A climb up Bolafjall above Bolungarvík to a skywalk platform cantilevered over the cliff edge.
  • Valagil A gentle walk into Álftafjörður to a narrow ravine and its striking waterfall.
  • Dynjandi A short walk up past a cascade of falls to the majestic Dynjandi, the Westfjords' tallest waterfall.

Pools & baths

  • Reykjafjarðarlaug A peaceful geothermal pool at the head of Reykjafjörður in Arnarfjörður.
  • Pollurinn Beloved hillside hot pots just outside the village of Tálknafjörður.
  • Krossneslaug One of Iceland's most remote pools, set on the beach in Strandir.
  • Drangsnes Hot Pots Three seaside hot tubs right in the middle of the village of Drangsnes.
  • Hellulaug An unspoiled natural pool by the sea in Vatnsfjörður, near Flókalundur.
  • Birkimelur A lovely little pool and natural hot pot on Barðaströnd overlooking Breiðafjörður.
  • Sundhöll Ísafjarðar A cozy indoor pool in the heart of Ísafjörður, open since 1945.
  • Sundlaug Bolungarvíkur A well-equipped pool in the coastal village of Bolungarvík.
  • Suðureyri Swimming Pool A cosy outdoor pool in a tiny fishing village on Súgandafjörður.
  • Flateyri Swimming Pool The village pool in Flateyri's sports centre on Önundarfjörður.
  • Þingeyri Swimming Pool Outdoor pool with hot tubs in Þingeyri on Dýrafjörður.
  • Patreksfjörður Swimming Pool Outdoor pool with a splendid view over Patreksfjörður fjord.
  • Tálknafjörður Swimming Pool Friendly village pool in Tálknafjörður with hot tubs.
  • Hólmavík Swimming Pool A 25 m pool with hot tubs and steam bath in the Strandir region.
  • Grettislaug at Reykhólar A 25 m geothermal outdoor pool at Reykhólar on Breiðafjörður.
  • Reykjanes Pool in Ísafjarðardjúp One of Iceland's longest pools, fed by free-flowing geothermal water.
  • Bíldudalur Swimming Pool Small village pool in the fishing hamlet of Bíldudalur on Arnarfjörður.
  • Gvendarlaug í Bjarnarfirði A pool at Laugarhóll in Bjarnarfjörður with an ancient natural spring linked to Guðmundur the Good.
  • Heydalur A small pool and hot tubs at the Heydalur country hotel in Mjóifjörður.
  • Djúpidalur A country pool and hot soak at the Djúpidalur travel service in Reykhólasveit.
