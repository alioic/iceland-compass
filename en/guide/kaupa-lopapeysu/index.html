Where to Buy a Real Icelandic Wool Sweater (Lopapeysa) and Authentic Craft

A guide to buying a genuine, hand-knitted lopapeysa and real Icelandic wool and craft — directly from the makers and cooperatives across the country.

What Makes a Lopapeysa Real?

The lopapeysa is Iceland's most iconic souvenir, but it is far more than that — it is a living craft tradition rooted in the Icelandic sheep itself. A genuine lopapeysa is knitted from lopi, a wool yarn spun from the fleece of the Icelandic sheep. This wool is unique: it has two layers, a coarse water-repellent outer hair (tog) and a soft insulating inner layer (þel). Together these fibres make the sweater warm, lightweight and remarkably weatherproof.

A classic lopapeysa is recognisable by its circular patterned yoke, which sits like a decorative collar across the shoulders, often in natural undyed sheep colours — white, grey, black and brown. The sweater is knitted in the round, in one piece and without seams, which is part of what makes it so durable. Although the yoke pattern is now seen as a national symbol, the design is actually fairly young, dating from the mid-20th century, but built on centuries-old wool traditions.

When you buy a genuine, hand-knitted lopapeysa, you are not just buying a garment — you are buying craftsmanship that often took days to knit, made from a raw material that comes straight from an Icelandic farm.

How to Tell Genuine from Imported Copies

Unfortunately the market is full of imported, machine-knitted copies that look like lopapeysur but are neither knitted in Iceland nor made from Icelandic wool. Here are a few ways to spot the real thing:

Check the label. A genuine hand-knitted sweater from the Icelandic Handknitting Association carries a certificate of origin and often the name or number of the knitter. Look for the words “hand-knitted in Iceland” or “100% Icelandic wool.” If the label only says “designed in Iceland,” it may well be manufactured abroad.

Feel the yarn. Real Icelandic lopi is slightly coarse and hairy to the touch, not silky-smooth. A very soft, uniform, low-priced sweater is probably made from imported wool or an acrylic blend.

Look at the knitting. In a hand-knitted sweater there are tiny, natural variations in the stitches and the yoke is knitted as one continuous piece. Perfectly uniform machine stitches and seams under the arms point to mass production.

Ask where it was knitted. In a real craft shop the staff can tell you where the wool comes from and sometimes even who knitted the sweater. If nobody can answer that, treat it as a warning sign.

The Wool Itself — the Heart of the Craft

To understand the lopapeysa you have to understand lopi. The Icelandic sheep has been isolated on the island for more than a thousand years, and its wool has evolved to withstand Icelandic weather. The fleece is sorted, carded and spun into various grades of lopi — from fine plate lopi to coarser types suited to different projects.

Some of the best purchases are not finished garments at all, but the wool itself. Across the country there are wool workshops and cooperatives where you can buy lopi directly, often in natural undyed sheep colours or hand-dyed with Icelandic plants. In some places you can follow the whole process, from fleece to yarn.

Buying the wool straight from the workshop is not only cheaper — it also supports Icelandic farming and small rural craft businesses. And if you knit yourself, you take home a story with your purchase, because you know exactly where the material came from.

Reykjavík and Nearby — Where to Start

In the capital area it is easy to find authentic craft if you know where to look.

The Icelandic Handknitting Association (Handprjónasamband Íslands) runs a shop in central Reykjavík where every sweater is hand-knitted by members from all over the country. This is the safest place to get a genuine, hand-knitted sweater with a certificate of origin, and the range of colours and sizes is wide. You can also buy lopi and knitting patterns to make your own.

Álafoss wool store in Mosfellsbær has worked with Icelandic wool since 1896 and is one of the most historic names in the country's wool story. It offers a broad selection of lopi, knitwear and sweaters in an old mill building just outside the city.

Farmers & Friends (Farmers Market) is an Icelandic design label that blends traditional wool with modern design — a great stop for anyone who wants Icelandic craft with a contemporary cut.

In Reykjanesbær, close to Keflavík Airport, Duus Handverk offers a varied selection of Icelandic craft — a handy last stop before you head home.

Around the Country — Direct from Knitters and Mills

Outside the capital is where you find the craft closest to its source, often in the very place where the wool is processed.

In West Iceland, Ullarselið at Hvanneyri is a wool centre where you can learn about the craft and buy directly. In South Iceland, near Selfoss, Þingborg Ullarvinnsla is a cooperative of knitters and wool workers selling hand-spun lopi and garments, while Hespuhúsið dyes wool with Icelandic plants using centuries-old methods. In Vík í Mýrdal, Víkurprjón (Icewear) is one of the largest knitting operations in the country, with a big selection.

In North Iceland, KIDKA Ullarverksmiðja in Hvammstangi is a working wool factory with a shop where you can see production and buy directly. In Skagafjörður, Atlantic Leather in Sauðárkrókur tans distinctive fish leather — a truly Icelandic material used in design and accessories. In Akureyri and Egilsstaðir, Flóra and Hús Handanna are shops combining Icelandic design and craft.

In the Westfjords, in Ísafjörður, Ívaf offers hand-knitted goods, and at Sólheimum in Grímsnes, Verslunin Vala sells work from the local craft community. Everywhere the same rule applies: buying directly from knitters and mills ensures the money reaches the people who actually made the item.

Frequently asked questions

How do I know a lopapeysa is genuine?

Look for a label confirming that the sweater is hand-knitted in Iceland from 100% Icelandic wool, ideally with the knitter's name or number. Real lopi feels coarse and hairy, the yoke is knitted as one seamless piece, and in a craft shop the staff can tell you where the wool came from and who knitted it. Shops such as the Icelandic Handknitting Association provide certificates of origin.

How much does a real lopapeysa cost?

The price varies and depends on the size, the pattern and whether the sweater is hand-knitted or machine-knitted. A hand-knitted sweater generally costs considerably more than a machine-made one, because it is a unique piece of craft that takes many hours — even days — to knit. You are paying for the knitter's time, genuine Icelandic wool, and a garment that lasts for decades. Very cheap “lopapeysur” are usually imported machine-knitted copies.

Can I buy the wool to knit my own?

Yes, and it is a great idea. The Icelandic Handknitting Association, Álafoss and many wool workshops such as Þingborg and KIDKA sell lopi directly, often along with patterns and needles. At places like Hespuhúsið you can even find plant-dyed lopi. Knitting your own sweater is cheaper and gives you a truly personal memento of Iceland.

Why should I buy directly from knitters and mills?

When you buy directly from the handknitting association, a wool mill or a cooperative, the payment goes to the people who actually made the item — knitters, farmers and small craft businesses, often in rural areas. You get an authentic product, you support Icelandic farming and craft traditions, and you usually take home a story that makes the purchase more personal.
