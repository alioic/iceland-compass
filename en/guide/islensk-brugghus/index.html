Iceland's Craft Breweries & Distilleries Worth Visiting

Beer was banned in Iceland until 1989 — today craft brewing thrives nationwide. A region-by-region guide to breweries and distilleries you can visit.

From Prohibition to a Thriving Craft Scene

Few countries have a beer history as strange as Iceland's. Strong beer — anything above 2.25% ABV — was banned here for more than seven decades and only became legal again on 1 March 1989. That date is still celebrated as “Beer Day” (Bjórdagurinn) and marks the real beginning of the beer culture Icelanders enjoy today.

What has happened in just over three decades is remarkable. From having no domestic craft beer at all, Iceland now boasts a whole ecosystem of small breweries, many of them in tiny fishing villages and rural districts far from the capital. The first true craft brewery, Bruggsmiðjan Kaldi, opened in 2006 in Árskógssandur on Eyjafjörður fjord, and it opened the door for other pioneers to follow.

Today you can find breweries in nearly every region, and many of them run taprooms or offer tours where you can taste the beer right where it is made. For travelers, this is a wonderful way to connect with locals and regional character — every brewery has its own story to tell.

What Makes Icelandic Craft Beer Special?

First and foremost, it is the water. Icelandic spring water is among the purest in the world, unfiltered and mineral-rich straight from the ground, and it forms the backbone of every beer. Many brewmasters say the water alone gives Icelandic beer its edge.

Then there is a growing love of local ingredients. Some breweries brew with Icelandic barley, crowberries, wild thyme, birch, rhubarb, seaweed or smoked malt to capture flavors rooted in the land itself. Ölvisholt Brugghús near Selfoss, for instance, became known for “Lava”, a smoked imperial stout inspired by the volcano Hekla, which is visible from the brewery's door.

Finally, there is regional character. A small brewery in Ísafjörður, the Westman Islands or Breiðdalsvík reflects its surroundings — beer names often nod to waterfalls, mountains, boats or local legends. This turns a beer trip around Iceland into something like a geography lesson with a glass in hand.

Reykjavík and the Southwest

Most travelers start in Reykjavík, and Ægir Brugghús is a good place to begin — a brewery in the heart of the city with a broad range of its own beers on tap. It is an easy way to get a feel for the styles before heading out into the countryside.

Just south of the city, in Garðabær, is Eimverk Distillery — Iceland's first whisky distillery. It produces Flóki single malt whisky from 100% Icelandic barley, along with VOR gin and brennivín. Eimverk offers tours with tastings, but book ahead, as visits are by appointment.

In Hveragerði, on the Golden Circle, Ölverk Pizza & Brugghús is especially interesting: the brewery is powered by geothermal energy, fitting for a town built entirely on geothermal heat. You can enjoy both pizza and beer brewed on-site. On a farm near Selfoss, the aforementioned Ölvisholt brews its well-known stouts, and in Borgarfjörður in the west sits Brugghús Steðja, a family brewery known for experimenting with unusual and seasonal ingredients.

The South Coast and the Islands

Heading east along the south coast, Vík í Mýrdal makes an ideal stop, where Smiðjan Brugghús is a popular destination. It is a brewpub and restaurant in one, with views over the black-sand beaches and beer brewed on the premises — a welcome break on a long drive.

Off the south coast lie the Westman Islands (Vestmannaeyjar), home to The Brothers Brewery, a small but ambitious operation. Taking the Herjólfur ferry out to the islands and ending the day with a local beer is an experience in itself — the islands are known for their strong sense of community, their birdlife and their puffins, and beer culture is part of that atmosphere.

These areas show how Icelandic breweries are often woven into tourism and food culture. Rather than being just a factory, the brewery is frequently a place where people gather, eat and talk.

North Iceland and the Westfjords

North Iceland is something of a cradle for Icelandic craft beer. In Árskógssandur on Eyjafjörður fjord you'll find Bruggsmiðjan Kaldi, the country's first craft brewery (established 2006), brewing in the Czech pilsner tradition. Right nearby is Bjórböðin, a beer spa where guests soak in tubs of young beer, hops and live yeast, and can even pour themselves a Kaldi from a tap beside the bath. It is one of the most distinctive experiences in the country.

In the herring town of Siglufjörður is Segull 67, a small family brewery in a former fish-processing house dating to 1934. The name refers to the compass needle that always points north, and inside the same building is Íshaf 67, a distillery producing gin and aquavit from local ingredients.

Farthest west, in Ísafjörður, Dokkan Brugghús opened the first brewery in the Westfjords — the nearest other one is more than 400 km away. Its beers carry names like Dynjandi IPA, after the famous waterfall, and the brewery sits close to the pier where cruise ships dock.

East Iceland — and Enjoying It Responsibly

East Iceland may be the best-kept secret of the country's beer scene. In Breiðdalsvík there is Beljandi Brugghús, a small brewery in a quiet fishing village, and in Egilsstaðir, Austri Brugghús runs the Askur Taproom, a comfortable spot to sit down and sample beer from the region. Farther east still, in the remote and beautiful Borgarfjörður eystri, is KHB Brugghús — proof that even the smallest communities can have a brewery of their own.

One thing is essential to remember: Iceland has a zero-tolerance policy on drink-driving. The law is strict and there is no safe lower limit. If you plan to taste beer, always have a designated driver, take a taxi, or arrange accommodation within walking distance.

The best way to support this growing culture is simple: visit the taprooms, buy beer directly from the breweries or from Vínbúðin, and enjoy in moderation. Every glass tells a story of pure water, local ingredients and a small community that decided to brew its own beer.

Frequently asked questions

Was beer really banned in Iceland?

Yes. Strong beer (above 2.25% ABV) was banned in Iceland for more than seven decades and did not become legal again until 1 March 1989, a date still known as Beer Day (Bjórdagurinn). The ban is one reason Iceland's craft-beer culture is relatively young but full of energy.

Can I visit breweries without booking a tour?

Often, yes. Many breweries run taprooms or restaurants where you can sit down and taste beer without a reservation. Formal guided tours with tastings, however, usually need to be booked in advance — Eimverk, for example. Always check opening hours, especially outside high season and in rural areas.

Where can I buy Icelandic craft beer?

Alcohol above 2.25% is not sold in supermarkets in Iceland. You buy beer at Vínbúðin (the state alcohol stores), directly from the breweries themselves where permitted, or enjoy it at licensed restaurants and bars.

Can I drive after tasting beer?

No. Iceland effectively has a zero-tolerance policy on drink-driving and penalties are severe. Plan your brewery trip so that you have a designated driver, take a taxi, or stay within walking distance of the brewery.
