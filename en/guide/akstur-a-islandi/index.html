Driving in Iceland: roads, F-roads, and safety

A practical guide to driving in Iceland: rules of the road, gravel and F-roads, weather resources, winter driving, and staying safe.

The basic rules of the road

In Iceland you drive on the right and overtake on the left. Headlights must be on at all times, day and night, all year round — this is the law, not a suggestion. Seatbelts are mandatory in every seat, front and back, and the driver is responsible for ensuring children are in appropriate car seats.

Speed limits are generally 90 km/h on paved rural roads, 80 km/h on gravel roads, and 50 km/h in built-up areas. Police monitor speed closely, speed cameras are common, and fines are steep. Remember that the safe speed is often well below the limit when conditions are poor — the sign is a maximum, not a target.

Driving under the influence of alcohol or drugs is treated very seriously and the legal limits are low. Using a handheld phone while driving is prohibited.

The single most important rule for Iceland's fragile nature: off-road driving is strictly illegal. Leaving marked roads and tracks — whether on moss, sand, in the highlands, or along the coast — causes damage that can take decades to heal and carries heavy fines. Always stay on marked roads and marked tracks.

Road types: Route 1, gravel, and F-roads

The Ring Road (Route 1) circles the country and is mostly paved, but for long stretches it is a single lane in each direction, not a motorway. Secondary and connecting roads may be paved or gravel, and their condition varies a great deal.

Gravel roads demand extra care. Loose gravel reduces grip, especially in bends and under braking, and you should slow down when meeting an oncoming car to avoid stones cracking windscreens. Where pavement changes to gravel there is often a sharp edge — slow right down at that transition.

F-roads are mountain roads into the highlands, marked with the letter F (for example F35 Kjölur, F26 Sprengisandur). They are for 4x4 vehicles only — this is a requirement, not a recommendation, and rental companies prohibit 2WD cars on F-roads in their terms. F-roads are open only in summer, often from June or July into autumn, with opening dates depending on conditions each year. Many F-roads include unbridged rivers that must be forded. River crossings are genuinely risky: depth and current change with weather and snowmelt, and if you do not know how to read a ford and cross it correctly, do not attempt it. Wait for another vehicle, watch where experienced drivers cross, and never drive across faster than walking pace.

2WD or 4x4 — and choosing the right car

Your choice of car matters more in Iceland than in most countries. A front-wheel-drive passenger car (2WD) is perfectly fine on the Ring Road and most paved secondary roads in summer, and suits travellers who stay in the lowlands. It cannot, however, legally or safely reach the highlands.

A four-wheel-drive vehicle (4x4) is essential if you plan to take F-roads, ford rivers, or travel in the highlands, and it gives more security in snow, ice, and wind in winter. A proper SUV with high ground clearance and low-range gearing is the right choice for the highlands; a small "soft-roader" is not always enough for deep river crossings.

It pays to match your rental to where you will actually go: a 4x4 for the highlands and winter trips, a smaller car for the summer Ring Road. The wrong car means either you cannot reach your destination or you take unnecessary risks — and many insurance policies are void if you drive on an F-road or through a river in a vehicle not built for it. Read the terms carefully before you book.

Weather, road conditions, and useful resources

Icelandic weather is changeable and can transform within an hour. Before you set off — and again along the way — check road conditions and the forecast. Three websites are indispensable: road.is (the Icelandic Road Administration) shows road status, closures, and highland openings; safetravel.is covers travel safety and lets you file a travel plan; and vedur.is (the Icelandic Met Office) provides forecasts and warnings.

Wind is an underrated hazard. Gusts can be strong enough to rip a car door out of your hands and bend the hinges — always hold the door firmly when opening it in high wind, and if possible position the car so the door opens on the sheltered side. Strong crosswinds can push vehicles, especially tall SUVs and campers, and sometimes the wisest move is to stop and wait out the worst gusts. On sand flats and near volcanoes, sandstorms and ash storms can carry fine grit that scours paint and glass and cuts visibility.

If a warning is issued or a road is closed, treat it not as advice but as a reason to rethink your plan. No destination is worth taking an unnecessary risk in severe weather.

Winter driving, single-lane bridges, and animals

In winter, daylight is very short — in December there are only a few hours of light — so much of your driving will be in the dark. Roads can be icy, snow-covered, or coated in hard-to-see black ice. Rental companies fit winter tyres (often studded) during the cold season; drive slower, keep a large gap, and expect braking distances to multiply on ice.

Single-lane bridges (signed "einbreið brú") are common, including on the Ring Road. The vehicle nearer the bridge has priority; slow down in good time, judge who arrives first, and stop if there is any doubt. Blind rises ("blindhæð") and blind bends are also frequent — keep well to the right and reduce speed, because an oncoming car may be sitting in the middle of the road.

Sheep roam free in summer and autumn and often stand beside or on the road. Lambs may suddenly bolt across to reach their mother. Slow down whenever you see sheep near the road and never assume they will stay put. Reindeer appear in the east. At fuel stations, self-service is the norm: most pumps take a card with a PIN, so make sure your card works with a PIN and carry a backup, as some automated pumps place a temporary hold on your account.

A short safety checklist

Before you set off: check road.is, vedur.is, and safetravel.is, and file a travel plan if you are heading somewhere remote.

Make sure the car matches the route — a 4x4 for F-roads, the highlands, and winter — and that your insurance covers what you intend to do.

Fill up with fuel before leaving main routes; petrol stations can be far apart, especially in the highlands and the Westfjords.

Carry water, snacks, warm clothing, and a phone charger. Headlights always on, everyone belted in.

Drive to the conditions, not just to the speed limit. Stay on marked roads — no off-road driving, ever.

If the weather turns suddenly: slow down, put all your lights on, and pull over somewhere safe if visibility disappears. It is always fine to postpone a trip.

With respect for the conditions, the right car, and a little preparation, driving in Iceland is very doable — and one of the best ways to experience the country on your own terms.

Frequently asked questions

Do I need a 4x4 to drive in Iceland?

Not for the Ring Road and lowlands in summer — a front-wheel-drive car handles those well. But a 4x4 is required for F-roads and the highlands, and adds safety in snow and ice in winter. Choose your car based on where you actually plan to go.

When do F-roads open?

F-roads are open only in summer, usually from June or July into autumn, but exact opening dates depend on snow and conditions each year. Always check road.is for up-to-date openings before you set off.

Can I drive off-road to get better photos?

No. Off-road driving is illegal everywhere in Iceland, causes long-lasting damage to nature, and carries heavy fines. Always stay on marked roads and tracks, regardless of the terrain or how tempting it looks.

What should I do if the weather turns suddenly?

Slow down, switch on all your lights, and keep a wide gap. If visibility disappears or the wind becomes dangerously strong, pull over somewhere safe and wait out the worst gusts. It is always better to postpone a trip than to take a risk.
