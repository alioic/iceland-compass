Farm shops & local food: buy straight from Icelandic producers

Buy fresh food and goods directly from Icelandic farms and small producers on your road trip — fresher, supports rural life, authentic.

What does 'beint frá býli' mean?

One of the most rewarding ways to get to know Iceland is through your stomach. As you drive the Ring Road you pass dairy farms, greenhouses, sheep pastures and small artisan producers — and many of them sell directly to visitors. This movement is called 'beint frá býli', literally 'direct from the farm', and it is all about connecting the person who eats the food with the person who grows or makes it, cutting out the middleman.

Why shop this way? First, the product is often fresher than anything you will find in a supermarket — the tomatoes were picked this morning, the ice cream made from the milk of the cows in the next stall. Second, your money goes straight to the family keeping the countryside alive. And third, it is simply a lovely experience: you meet the farmer, see the animals, hear the story behind the cheese or the salt. This is travel that leaves something behind — for you and for the people who live there.

What can you find on Icelandic farms?

The range surprises many people. Dairy farms offer homemade ice cream and creamy skyr, often served on the spot with berries or warm sauce. Sheep and goat farmers sell lamb, smoked meat and handmade cheeses. In greenhouse districts such as the south, geothermal heat means tomatoes, cucumbers, peppers and even strawberries grow year-round.

Then there is everything else: homemade jam and cordial from Icelandic berries, honey from small apiaries, hand-harvested sea salt from the Westfjords, fresh and smoked fish straight off the boat, barley and organic vegetables, and crafts like wool goods and soaps. Many farms also run a little café where you can sit down, have a waffle with rhubarb jam and a cup of coffee, and enjoy the view. In other words, you are not just buying food — you are buying a moment.

Farms and markets in the south and west

In the south, in the heart of greenhouse country, Sólskinsbúðin at Flúðir is an ideal stop — a small market where you buy vegetables grown right in the district. In Hveragerði, the greenhouse town itself, Skyrgerðin makes handcrafted skyr in the old-fashioned style, well worth a taste.

In the west, Ljómalind local market in Borgarnes is a favourite for anyone who wants to shop directly from the region's farmers under one roof — everything from cheese and meat to jam and crafts. To take the experience further, Saltverk on Reykjanes in the Westfjords hand-harvests sea salt using geothermal heat, the same method used in Iceland for centuries. In Reykjavík itself, Búrið is a dedicated cheese shop and pantry for Icelandic artisan goods, and Kolaportið, the old flea market down by the harbour, is the place to sample dried fish, fermented shark, rye bread and other traditional fare.

North and east: ice cream, cheese and coffee over the cowshed

The north is a paradise for food lovers. In Eyjafjörður, Holtsel makes its celebrated homemade ice cream, Holtsels-Hnoss, from the milk of its own cows. Nearby is Kaffi Kú, a café that stands literally on top of a cowshed — you look down at the cattle while you eat beef raised on the farm. At Lake Mývatn, Vogafjós welcomes guests with a farm café and a small boutique offering homemade cheese, smoked trout and other local treats.

In Fljót, west of Siglufjörður, Brúnastaðir crafts fine goat cheese. Far out on the edge of the highlands, at Möðrudalur, Fjalladýrð is a remote farm with a café and shop where you can buy home baking and refuel on your journey across the interior. In the east, Móðir Jörð at Vallanes is an organic farm with a shop selling barley, rapeseed oil, vegetables and other organic produce straight from the field.

Village shops and roadside stops worth pulling over for

Part of the countryside's charm lies in the good old rural shops and co-ops. On the Ring Road in Hrútafjörður, Staðarskáli is a classic stop — generations of Icelanders have filled the tank and grabbed a hot dog or a doughnut there. Just up the road, at Laugarbakki, Langafit handicraft market lets local people sell knitwear, jam and artwork.

In the Strandir region, the Kaupfélag Steingrímsfjarðar co-op in Hólmavík, together with Pakkhúsið, makes a charming mix of shop and history. And in Þingeyri on Dýrafjörður, Simbahöllin is a cosy café in an old building known for its Belgian waffles and warm atmosphere. In Suðureyri on Súgandafjörður, Fisherman focuses on fresh fish straight from the fjord and a genuine fishing-village feel. Bear in mind that many of these places are seasonal — it pays to check opening hours before you set off, especially outside the peak summer months.

Practical tips for the road

Carry some cash as well as a card — many small farms take cards but not all, and mobile signal can be patchy in the countryside. Keep a cool bag in the car if you plan to buy cheese, meat or fish to take with you; much of it does not keep well in a warm vehicle.

Plan your stops loosely but stay flexible — part of the fun is spotting a sign that says 'ice cream' or 'eggs for sale' and swinging off the road. Be considerate: these are people's homes and workplaces, so respect opening hours and closed-off areas. And finally, do not hesitate to ask — rural Icelanders are usually proud of their product and happy to tell you the story behind it.

Frequently asked questions

What does 'beint frá býli' mean?

It literally means buying food directly from the farmer or producer with no middleman — for example ice cream, skyr, cheese, meat, vegetables or jam made on the farm itself. The idea is about freshness, transparency and supporting rural communities.

Can I just turn up at farm shops?

Usually yes, at places that have a shop or café open during posted hours. Some smaller producers, however, only receive visitors by arrangement, so it is worth checking a website or Facebook page before driving a long way.

Are farm shops open in winter?

Many are seasonal and open mainly from spring through autumn, when tourism peaks. Some close entirely in the depths of winter or cut their hours sharply. Always check opening times in advance outside summer, especially at more remote spots.

Is buying directly from the farm expensive?

Prices vary by product and place, but you are paying for quality, freshness and supporting small producers. Often you get something you simply cannot buy in shops — and the experience itself is part of the value.
