The South Coast of Iceland: The Definitive Self-Drive Guide

The South Coast: Iceland's most popular drive

The South Coast, or Suðurströndin, is without question Iceland's busiest and best-loved self-drive route, and for good reason. Along a single straight run east on Route 1, you pass waterfalls, glaciers, black-sand beaches, sea stacks and canyons, each more dramatic than the last, with glaciers and mountains framing the view almost the whole way. Few roads anywhere pack so much into so short a distance, and the best part is that it suits everyone: families, photographers, hikers, and travellers who simply want to drive east and drink in the scenery.

The route runs from Reykjavík east across the South, through Hvolsvöllur and beneath the Eyjafjöll cliffs, past Seljalandsfoss and Skógafoss, out onto the Sólheimasandur plains, on to Dyrhólaey and Reynisfjara, through the hub town of Vík í Mýrdal, and further east via Kirkjubæjarklaustur, Skaftafell and finally to Jökulsárlón glacier lagoon and Diamond Beach. You can go as far east as your time allows and turn back, or continue on around the full Ring Road.

This guide walks through every major stop from west to east, tells you honestly how long you really need, when to go, where it makes sense to sleep and eat, and, most importantly of all, the safety points you must not ignore. It is meant as a thorough, honest and practical handbook, written from a local perspective by people who know the road well.

Getting there and how to drive it

By far the best way to experience the South Coast is in your own rental car. You set the pace, stop wherever you like and linger where you want, something a bus tour can never fully offer. The driving itself is simple: you head almost continuously east on Route 1, the Ring Road, and the road is paved the entire way to Jökulsárlón and beyond. You do not need a four-wheel-drive vehicle for this classic route, though one adds a margin of safety in winter.

To give a sense of distances and driving times from Reykjavík, with no stops: Seljalandsfoss is about 120 km away, roughly 1 hour and 45 minutes. Vík í Mýrdal is about 185 km, roughly 2.5 hours. Jökulsárlón is about 370 km, which takes roughly 5 hours one way. These are rough figures without stops; in reality you should add several hours once you factor in time at the sights, which is the whole point of the drive.

In winter, weather and road conditions can change these times dramatically. Always check road.is for conditions and vedur.is for the forecast before you set off, and give yourself a generous buffer. Fill up in Reykjavík or in Selfoss, because fuel stations become sparser the further east you go. The main service towns along the way are Hvolsvöllur, Vík and Kirkjubæjarklaustur.

How long do you need?

This is the most important question, and the one most people underestimate. The honest answer depends on how far east you want to reach.

If you only have one day, you can comfortably reach the highlights west of Vík: Seljalandsfoss, Skógafoss, Dyrhólaey and Reynisfjara, finishing in Vík. It is a long day, often ten to twelve hours including the round-trip drive, but it is realistic and rewarding. Just do not try to squeeze Jökulsárlón into a day trip from Reykjavík; that is roughly 740 km there and back, and you would spend the entire day behind the wheel.

To reach Jökulsárlón at a reasonable pace you really need two days, with an overnight in Vík or Kirkjubæjarklaustur for the first night. That lets you explore the western sights on day one and drive east to the lagoon and Skaftafell on day two. Three days, though, is ideal, because it leaves room for a glacier walk, a longer hike, or simply enjoying the coast without constantly rushing. Remember that the South Coast is about stopping, not speeding past.

Seljalandsfoss and hidden Gljúfrabúi

Seljalandsfoss is often the first big stop, about 120 km from Reykjavík, and it is genuinely special: a waterfall roughly 60 metres tall that tumbles over an old sea cliff, and what makes it unique is that you can walk behind it. A path loops into the alcove behind the falling water and gives an unforgettable perspective. Be ready to get wet, because the spray is very real, and bring a waterproof jacket and sturdy shoes, as the path turns slippery, especially in winter when it may be closed due to ice.

What many visitors miss is Gljúfrabúi, a hidden waterfall just a few hundred metres north along the cliff. It plunges inside a narrow rock cleft, and to see it properly you have to wade a short stream into the gorge. Your feet will get wet, but the reward is a waterfall inside its own rock chamber, often almost empty while the crowds gather at Seljalandsfoss. Allow about 45 minutes to an hour for both. There is a paid parking fee at the lot.

Skógafoss and the Fimmvörðuháls trail

About 30 km further east rises Skógafoss, one of Iceland's most majestic waterfalls: roughly 60 metres tall and about 25 metres wide, and it falls with such force that the spray regularly throws up rainbows in sunshine. You can walk right up to its base across flat sand, mind the spray that carries surprisingly far, and feel the power of it in your chest.

On the left, a steep metal staircase of around 370 steps climbs to the top of the falls, where the view over the plains and out to sea is spectacular. That staircase is also the start of the famous Fimmvörðuháls trail, one of Iceland's most popular hikes, which runs between the Eyjafjallajökull and Mýrdalsjökull glaciers and ends in Þórsmörk. It is a full day's walk for experienced hikers and only in summer; most visitors simply climb to the viewpoint and back. Allow about 45 minutes to an hour for the stop itself.

Sólheimajökull glacier and the Sólheimasandur plane wreck

A short side road just east of Skógar leads in to Sólheimajökull, a glacier tongue reaching down from the Mýrdalsjökull ice cap. This is one of the most accessible places in Iceland to walk on a glacier with a guide, wearing crampons and carrying an ice axe, an experience many rank as a highlight of the trip. Never venture onto the ice alone; crevasses are deadly, and the glacier should only be walked with certified guides. Sólheimajökull is also a sobering illustration of climate change, as it has retreated visibly in recent years and the glacier snout now sits noticeably further from the car park than it once did.

Just to the east, out on the Sólheimasandur plain, lies one of Iceland's most famous photographic subjects: the wreck of a US DC-3 military plane that made an emergency landing in 1973, with everyone on board surviving. The wreck rests on the jet-black, perfectly flat sand and is strikingly photogenic. Be prepared to walk, though: it is roughly 3.8 km each way from the car park by the main road, across a completely flat, shelterless expanse where the wind bites. Allow about two and a half to three hours for the round trip on foot. A shuttle runs back and forth for those who would rather skip the walk. There is no shelter and no services out on the sand, so dress warmly.

Dyrhólaey: the arch and the puffins

Dyrhólaey is a striking promontory that rises out of the flatlands just west of Vík, crowned by an old lighthouse. Its name refers to the huge rock arch that stands out in the sea, an opening so large that a small plane has flown through it. The view from the headland is magnificent: long black beaches stretching in both directions, the Reynisdrangar sea stacks to the east and glacier to the north.

In summer, typically from mid-May into August, puffins nest in the grassy slopes of the headland, and this is one of the best places in the South to see these beloved seabirds up close. Note that part of the area can be closed temporarily during the nesting season to protect the birds, and the upper section of the headland is sometimes only accessible by four-wheel-drive or closed in high winds. Respect all closures; they are there to protect both the wildlife and you. Allow about 30 to 45 minutes.

Reynisfjara: the black beach and its deadly waves

SAFETY FIRST: Reynisfjara is one of Iceland's most beautiful beaches, and also one of its most dangerous. Here so-called sneaker waves, or sleeper waves, surge far up the beach without warning, reaching many times further than the waves before them. They are freezing, sudden and immensely powerful, and people have drowned here. Never turn your back on the sea, never go near the water's edge, and stay well above the line where the ocean has wetted the sand. Watch for the warning lights and signs. This is not an exaggeration, so take it seriously.

That said, Reynisfjara is unforgettable. The jet-black sand, the crashing surf and the perfectly formed basalt columns in the cliff wall, where people love to sit as if on giant steps, create an almost otherworldly setting. Out in the surf rise the Reynisdrangar sea stacks, tall rock pinnacles that folklore says are trolls turned to stone when the sun came up as they hauled a ship to shore.

Come early in the day if you can, because this is one of the busiest beaches in the whole South and the car park fills quickly. Allow about 45 minutes to an hour. There is a cafe by the car park. And remember: never let the beauty make you forget the sea.

Vík í Mýrdal: the hub of the route

Vík í Mýrdal is Iceland's southernmost village and the natural hub of the South Coast, about 185 km from Reykjavík. Despite its small size, with only a few hundred residents, Vík is the most important service point on the route: it has fuel stations, a grocery store, restaurants, accommodation and outdoor shops. For many travellers, Vík is the natural place to spend the first night on a two-day trip.

Above the village stands the white church, Reyniskirkja, on a hill with a lovely view over the town, the beach and the Reynisdrangar stacks. The beach at Vík itself is also black and dramatic, but keep in mind that the same dangerous waves as at Reynisfjara can surge here too. Towering above the village is the Mýrdalsjökull ice cap, and beneath it lies the Katla volcano, one of Iceland's most active. Vík is a good place to fill up, eat and rest before heading further east, because services grow sparser from here on.

East to Fjaðrárgljúfur, Kirkjubæjarklaustur and Skaftafell

East of Vík the landscape changes. You cross vast black sand plains, Mýrdalssandur and later Skeiðarársandur, shaped over centuries by glacial floods. It is a desolate but magnificent landscape that few forget.

A short way west of Kirkjubæjarklaustur is Fjaðrárgljúfur, a deep, serpentine canyon with mossy, almost fairytale-like walls and a river winding along its floor. A footpath runs along the rim to viewpoints. The canyon has at times been closed temporarily to protect fragile vegetation, so respect all signage and stay on the marked path. Allow about 45 minutes to an hour.

Kirkjubæjarklaustur, often simply called Klaustur, is a small service centre with a fuel station, accommodation and dining, and it makes an ideal overnight base for those who want to reach Jökulsárlón the next day. From there you continue east to Skaftafell, part of Vatnajökull National Park, the largest national park in Europe. Skaftafell is a hiker's paradise with many marked trails and glorious glacier views. The most famous walk leads to Svartifoss, a waterfall that falls over dark, regular basalt columns resembling the pipes of an organ, which helped inspire the architecture of Reykjavík's Hallgrímskirkja church. The hike to Svartifoss and back from the visitor centre is about 1.5 km each way and takes roughly 1.5 hours round trip, fairly steep but well worth it.

Jökulsárlón and Diamond Beach

The crowning highlight of the eastern stretch, about 370 km from Reykjavík, is Jökulsárlón, a glacier lagoon where icebergs break off the snout of the Breiðamerkurjökull glacier and drift slowly toward the sea. The water is deep blue, the icebergs run through every shade of white, blue and clear, and you can often see seals swimming among them. It is one of Iceland's most spectacular places, and many travellers rate it the highlight of the entire trip. In summer, boat tours are available, both on large amphibious boats and small zodiacs, weaving between the icebergs, and it is an unforgettable experience.

Across the road, where the lagoon empties into the ocean, lies Breiðamerkursandur, better known as Diamond Beach. There the icebergs wash ashore and lie like giant glittering diamonds on the jet-black sand, especially striking in low sunlight. No two days are alike, because the ice is forever changing. Allow at least one to two hours for the lagoon and beach together. The same safety rules apply here as at Reynisfjara: stay back from the water's edge, never climb onto the icebergs, as they can roll, and keep a close watch on the sea.

The best time of year to go

The South Coast is a year-round route, but the seasons offer very different experiences, and it is only honest to name both the upsides and the drawbacks.

Summer (June to August) is the easiest time. The days are almost endless with the midnight sun, all roads and tracks are open, the weather is milder, and the puffins are at Dyrhólaey. It is also the peak season, so the main sites like Reynisfjara and Seljalandsfoss get crowded. The fix is simple: go early in the morning or late in the evening, since it stays light around the clock.

Winter (November to March) is more dramatic but more demanding. There is the chance of the northern lights when the sky is clear, and you can join organised ice-cave tours into the blue glaciers, a magnificent experience available only in the coldest months. On the other hand, daylight is short, only a few hours in December, the weather can change fast, and driving conditions can get difficult. If you drive in winter, go carefully, check road.is daily, allow plenty of time, and be ready to change your plans. Spring and autumn are a fine middle ground, with fewer tourists but changeable weather.

Where to stay and eat along the way

Accommodation is spread along the route, but demand is high in summer, so book well ahead, often many months in advance for the peak season. Three places make natural bases. Hvolsvöllur and its surroundings work well for a first night if you want to be near Seljalandsfoss and Skógafoss. Vík í Mýrdal is central and the most popular overnight stop, with a good range of lodging and restaurants. Kirkjubæjarklaustur is the smartest choice if your goal is to reach Jökulsárlón the next day, as it shortens the eastward drive considerably.

Options range from campsites and guesthouses up to hotels and countryside farm stays, and many places include breakfast. Food along the way is simple but good: soups, lamb, fish and often Icelandic pastries. Fuel stations and small shops serve fast food and are frequently the only option between villages, so it is wise to carry some snacks, water and supplies in the car. I avoid naming specific businesses or prices here, because availability changes quickly, so look up the latest reviews and book directly.

Safety and practical tips

The South Coast is safe if you respect nature, but it does not forgive carelessness. The most important point bears repeating: at Reynisfjara, and at the sea elsewhere along the route, dangerous sneaker waves surge far up the shore without warning. Never turn your back on the ocean and stay well back from the water's edge.

The weather changes quickly in Iceland. Sun and calm can turn to wind and rain in moments, so dress in layers and keep a waterproof outer shell. The wind is a particular hazard: hold on tightly to your car door when you open it, because a gust can tear it off its hinges in an instant, which is the most common damage to rental cars in Iceland. Park with the nose of the car into the wind where you can.

Before you set off, and daily on the road, check three sites: safetravel.is for safety advice and to leave a travel plan, vedur.is for the forecast and wind warnings, and road.is for conditions and closures. Fill the tank whenever you can, because the distances between fuel stations are long east of Vík. Finally, off-road driving is strictly forbidden and carries heavy fines, because fragile moss and vegetation take decades to recover. Respect all closures, stay on marked paths, and remember that much of the land is privately owned by farmers. Treat the country with respect, and it will treat you well in return.

Frequently asked questions

Can you drive the South Coast in one day?

Yes, you can reach the main sights west of Vík in one long day: Seljalandsfoss, Skógafoss, Dyrhólaey and Reynisfjara, finishing in Vík. Allow ten to twelve hours including the round-trip drive. Do not, however, try to reach Jökulsárlón the same day from Reykjavík; that is roughly 740 km there and back and far too much for a day trip.

Do I need a four-wheel-drive car?

No, not for the classic route. Route 1 is paved all the way to Jökulsárlón and beyond, so an ordinary car is fine in summer. In winter a four-wheel-drive adds safety on snow and ice, and for some side tracks, such as the upper section of Dyrhólaey, it can be necessary.

How far is Jökulsárlón from Reykjavík?

Jökulsárlón is about 370 km from Reykjavík, which takes roughly 5 hours one way without stops. Because of the distance, it is strongly recommended to spend at least one night along the way, for example in Vík or Kirkjubæjarklaustur, rather than attempting a day trip.

Why is Reynisfjara considered dangerous?

At Reynisfjara, so-called sneaker waves surge far up the beach without warning, reaching many times further than the waves before them. They are freezing and immensely powerful, and people have drowned there. Never turn your back on the sea, stay well above the wet sand, and watch for the warning lights.

When can you see puffins at Dyrhólaey?

Puffins nest at Dyrhólaey through the summer, typically from mid-May into August. They leave the country in late summer once the chicks can fly. Note that part of the area can be closed temporarily during the nesting season to protect the birds.

Can you visit ice caves on the South Coast?

Yes, but only in the coldest months, usually from November into March, when the glacier ice is stable enough. The tours are always led by certified guides, often departing from Jökulsárlón or Skaftafell. Never enter an ice cave or step onto a glacier alone; it is life-threatening.

How long is the walk to the Sólheimasandur plane wreck?

It is roughly 3.8 km each way from the car park by the main road, across a completely flat, shelterless expanse, so allow about two and a half to three hours for the round trip on foot. A shuttle runs back and forth for those who prefer to skip the walk. Dress warmly, as there is no shelter and no services out on the sand.

What should I bring?

A waterproof outer layer and shoes, warm clothing in layers, and a hat and gloves even in summer, because the weather changes fast. Carry water, snacks and a full tank of fuel. For walks such as the plane wreck or Svartifoss, good hiking shoes are essential.
